Case 032
Case Classification
Primary phenotype: Binocular / depth perception dysfunction phenotype
Secondary phenotype: None
Primary diagnosis: Left sixth nerve palsy (recent onset)
Modifiers: diabetes; recurrent falls history; multifocal / progressive use outdoors
Demographics
Age: 80
Gender: Male
Care setting: community optometry practice following GP referral
Falls pathway / referral context: referred after recurrent falls outdoors; GP noted intermittent double vision and difficulty judging kerb height.
History
Chief complaint: “Things separate side by side when I look into the distance.”
Ocular history: Three-month history of binocular horizontal diplopia, worse at distance and in left gaze, with two confirmed falls on uneven footpaths in the last 4 months. He closes one eye when crossing car parks and feels less steady when stepping off kerbs. Uses bifocals full-time. No previous history of strabismus and no headache, ptosis, or acute neurological symptoms. He lives with his wife and has reduced walking outside alone.
General medical history (including relevant medications): 
Type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolaemia; 

Medications: metformin, antihypertensive therapy, and statin.
Driving status: Stopped driving after diplopia became frequent.

Periphery: Dilated examination unremarkable OU
Clinical Impression
Left sixth nerve palsy, likely microvascular aetiology, causing a binocular vision / depth perception dysfunction phenotype. 
Distance diplopia and loss of functional stereopsis are the main visual contributors to recurrent outdoor falls.
Management Plan
Urgent GP communication regarding likely microvascular cranial nerve palsy and need for systemic work up including blood pressure, hbA1c, fasting blood glucose, serum total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, ESR, and CRP.
Temporary occlusion or Fresnel prism discussed for safer mobility pending recovery.
Advised not to drive while diplopia is present.
Recommended single-vision distance correction rather than bifocals for walking tasks.
Review in 12 weeks to monitor alignment and symptom change, or sooner if new neurological symptoms develop.

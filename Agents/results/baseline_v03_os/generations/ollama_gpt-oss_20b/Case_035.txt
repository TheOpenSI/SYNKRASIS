==============================
Case 035
==============================


1. **Patient Context**  
   • 67‑year‑old male, active gym user, driving daytime and occasional dusk trips.  
   • Primary diagnosis: moderate primary open‑angle glaucoma OU with progressive inferior visual field loss (MD –7.0 dB OD, –8.0 dB OS; VFI 84–86 %).  
   • Key visual complaints: delayed detection of floor‑level obstacles, especially in dim hallways; near‑misses reported but no confirmed falls.  
   • Ocular history: stable on latanoprost, no central acuity loss, reduced contrast sensitivity (1.35 log units), orthophoria distance, 4 ° near exophoria, 80″ stereo.  
   • Relevant medical history: hypertension, obstructive sleep apnoea.  
   • Care setting: shared‑care glaucoma review in community optometry; ophthalmology follow‑up scheduled in months.  

2. **Key Vision Issues**  
   • **Peripheral field loss (inferior defects)** → impaired obstacle detection at floor level.  
   • **Reduced contrast sensitivity** → difficulty distinguishing low‑contrast floor markers, especially in low light.  
   • **Visual field variability with light levels** → darker hallways increase risk of near‑misses.  
   • **No central blur or diplopia** → distance and near tasks largely preserved; visual impairment primarily spatial.  

3. **Functional Impact**  

   *Mobility and Falls*  
   – Risk of stepping onto unseen low furniture or uneven floor surfaces; near‑misses in dim corridors.  
   – Possible challenges negotiating stairs/steps if low‑contrast or cluttered.  

   *ADLs/IADLs*  
   – Walking in the home, especially during evening or low‑light conditions.  
   – Kitchen and bathroom navigation (doorways, sinks, bathtubs).  
   – No documented issues with medication management or reading; central acuity adequate.  

   *Participation / Confidence / Roles*  
   – Continued gym attendance and potential social activities; confidence in walking may be reduced during dusk or poorly lit areas.  

   *Environmental Interaction*  
   – Low‑light environments, floor clutter, inconsistent lighting, low‑contrast floor surfaces.  

4. **Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**  

   **Assessment Before Prescribing**  
   – *Home environment assessment:* lighting (intensity, uniformity, glare), floor clutter, placement of furniture, consistent use of high‑contrast floor markers.  
   – *Mobility assessment:* gait speed, stride length, obstacle detection, stair negotiation, reaction to low‑contrast markers.  
   – *Equipment check:* verify that distance spectacles are worn consistently when walking; assess suitability of frames for stability.  

   **Environmental Modifications / Home Environment**  
   – Recommend installing additional task lighting (e.g., LED strip lights along hallways, motion‑activated floor lights).  
   – Encourage removal or repositioning of low‑profile furniture, rugs, or cords that could be overlooked.  
   – Use contrasting floor tape or high‑contrast carpet edging to delineate thresholds, steps, and stair risers.  

   **Lighting and Contrast**  
   – Ensure even illumination in hallways and stairways; avoid harsh glare from overhead fixtures.  
   – Use warmer colour temperatures (2700–3000 K) to reduce contrast loss at dusk.  

   **Mobility Strategies**  
   – *Orientation & Mobility referral:* for training in scanning techniques, safe route planning, and use of a non‑visual cane if indicated.  
   – Encourage head‑turn scanning before stepping; practice in low‑light conditions under supervision.  

   **Assistive Technology / Non‑Visual Compensation**  
   – Consider low‑light headlamps or wearable LED lights to increase ambient illumination during evening activities.  
   – Explore smartphone apps that provide audible alerts for obstacles or floor transitions (e.g., “Be My Eyes” or “BlindSquare”).  

   **Task Adaptations**  
   – *Kitchen and bathroom:* place tactile markers (e.g., textured strips) at door thresholds, near sink, and on bathtub edges.  
   – *Walking routes:* identify “safe” pathways that minimize turns and use of staircases; consider a dedicated walking path with high‑contrast boundaries.  

   **Education and Support**  
   – Educate patient on consistent spectacle wear when walking; discuss potential benefit of higher‑power lenses for contrast enhancement if indicated by optometrist.  
   – Instruct on maintaining clear walkways and consistent lighting; advise family/carer to avoid sudden changes in room layout.  

   **Driving / Transport**  
   – Discuss increased risk of low‑level hazards at dusk; recommend a brief functional driving assessment if patient experiences difficulty.  
   – Suggest planning trips for well‑lit times, using familiar routes, and maintaining vehicle interior lighting.  

   **Clinical Follow‑Up / Communication**  
   – Coordinate with ophthalmology for IOP management; share OT observations on functional safety to inform future treatment decisions.  
   – Re‑evaluate functional mobility and home environment after any change in vision status or after O&M training.  

5. **Falls Risk Level**  
   – **Moderate** – progressive peripheral field loss combined with reduced contrast sensitivity and reported near‑misses in low‑light conditions create a measurable, but not immediate, fall risk.  

6. **Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings**  
   • Assess and modify home lighting and floor clutter to enhance obstacle detection.  
   • Evaluate gait and obstacle‑detection ability; consider O&M training for scanning and safe route planning.  
   • Provide education on consistent spectacle use and low‑light adaptive strategies.  
   • Monitor functional driving safety and support continued independent mobility.

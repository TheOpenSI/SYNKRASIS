==============================
Case 072
==============================


**1. Patient Context**  
- 82‑year‑old female, living independently with occasional family support.  
- No driving; has had one recent stumble in the kitchen.  
- Primary ocular diagnosis: advanced dry AMD with bilateral geographic atrophy → central vision loss phenotype.  
- Vision: distance 6/30 OU, near N12 habitual, high‑add trial N6 possible.  
- Peripheral fields preserved; central fixation impaired; contrast sensitivity not testable.  
- Medical comorbidities: hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, osteoporosis (on antihypertensive therapy and statin).  
- No recurrent falls; single fall in kitchen identified by GP.


**2. Key Vision Issues**  

- **Central acuity loss (≈6/30)** → difficulty reading small print, recognizing faces, and performing fine‑motor tasks.  
- **Impaired near vision (N12 habitual)** → challenges with medication labels, food‑prep instructions, and reading household texts.  
- **Central fixation difficulty + reduced contrast sensitivity** → difficulty locating low‑contrast or patterned objects directly ahead (e.g., dark saucepan on patterned mat).  
- **Preserved peripheral fields** but loss of central vision increases risk of tripping or colliding with objects placed directly in the forward visual field.


**3. Functional Impact**

| Domain | Potential Implications (documented or inferred) |
|--------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Mobility & Falls** | May stumble on low‑contrast or patterned surfaces; recent kitchen stumble suggests risk of tripping when objects lie directly ahead. |
| **ADLs/IADLs** | Medication management (reading labels), meal preparation (reading recipe or labels, cutting food, locating utensils), reading receipts/labels, and general house‑keeping tasks that rely on near vision. |
| **Participation / Confidence / Roles** | Reduced confidence in cooking and food‑prep; face‑recognition difficulty may affect social interactions. |
| **Environmental Interaction** | Lighting levels, surface contrast, patterned flooring, and placement of low‑contrast kitchenware directly ahead are risk factors. |


**4. Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**

*Self‑Care*  
- **Assessment**: Evaluate whether near‑vision difficulties interfere with daily medication intake and grooming.  
- **Intervention**: Teach use of a hand‑held magnifier or high‑add spectacles for reading labels; consider high‑contrast labeling or sticker use on medication bottles.

*Household Management*  
- **Assessment**: Observe meal‑preparation workflow to identify steps where central vision loss increases fall risk (e.g., locating cutting board, knives).  
- **Intervention**:  
  - Establish consistent kitchenware placement to reduce the need for forward scanning.  
  - Use tactile markers (e.g., raised stickers) on frequently used items.  
  - Encourage “step‑by‑step” task analysis for cooking tasks.

*Environmental Modifications / Home Environment*  
- **Assessment**: Conduct a home lighting and contrast audit, focusing on kitchen and living areas.  
- **Intervention**:  
  - Increase overall illumination (task lighting in kitchen, ambient lighting in living area).  
  - Use high‑contrast floor strips or non‑patterned mats to delineate safe walking paths.  
  - Remove or secure loose rugs, cords, and clutter from high‑traffic areas.  
  - Where feasible, replace patterned floor coverings with low‑contrast alternatives.

*Mobility Strategies*  
- **Assessment**: Observe navigation within the kitchen and other rooms; identify use of forward‑looking vs. side‑looking scanning.  
- **Intervention**:  
  - Recommend orientation & mobility (O&M) referral for personalized scanning training and route planning.  
  - Explore use of a cane or wand to provide tactile cues in low‑contrast environments if appropriate.  

*Task Adaptations*  
- **Assessment**: Evaluate how the patient currently performs near‑vision tasks (e.g., reading labels, cutting food).  
- **Intervention**:  
  - Use a magnifier holder or stand for stationary tasks.  
  - Introduce larger‑print recipe cards or digital audio instructions.  
  - Encourage preparation of meals in a dedicated, well‑lit area to reduce the need for rapid visual assessment.

*Assistive Technology / Non‑visual Compensation*  
- **Assessment**: Determine comfort and skill with devices (e.g., smartphone reading apps, voice‑assistant).  
- **Intervention**:  
  - Trial smartphone or tablet apps that magnify text and read aloud.  
  - Provide audio reminders for medication schedules.  

*Education and Support*  
- **Patient & Family**:  
  - Educate on the importance of consistent lighting and high‑contrast cues.  
  - Discuss safe kitchen practices (e.g., keep cookware in fixed locations, use tactile markers).  
  - Review strategies for safe navigation in the home (clear pathways, avoid patterned mats).  
- **Safety**: Encourage use of a “stop‑light” (red‑light/stop) system for kitchen tasks that are particularly hazardous when vision is limited.

*Clinical Follow‑up / Communication*  
- Coordinate with low‑vision specialist to ensure that magnification devices and lighting recommendations are integrated into the patient’s visual care plan.  
- Reassess functional impact after the 4‑month retinal review or sooner if new visual changes occur.


**5. Falls Risk Level**  
- **High** – central vision loss, single fall, and documented tripping on low‑contrast surfaces indicate a significant vision‑related fall risk that warrants proactive assessment and intervention.

**6. Summary of Key OT Considerations**  
- Prioritize safety‑focused environmental modifications (lighting, contrast, removal of patterned mats).  
- Target medication management and meal‑prep tasks for task‑specific adaptations and assistive technology use.  
- Facilitate O&M assessment to address forward‑scanning challenges and reduce tripping risk.  
- Provide patient‑and‑family education on consistent visual cues and safe kitchen practices to support independent participation.

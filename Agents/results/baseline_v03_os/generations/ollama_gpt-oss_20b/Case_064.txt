==============================
Case 064
==============================


1. **Patient Context**  
- 82‑year‑old male, currently receiving outpatient rehabilitation after a right occipital stroke.  
- Experienced one fall shortly after discharge when he missed a left‑handed doorway.  
- Not driving; on anticoagulation and antihypertensive medication.  
- Engaged in OT and physiotherapy for mobility retraining.

2. **Key Vision Issues**  
- **Left homonymous hemianopia** – loss of vision in the left visual field, evident by doorway collisions and difficulty locating the start of lines when reading.  
- **Reduced contrast sensitivity (1.35 log units)** – may diminish ability to detect low‑contrast objects or edges in cluttered or dim indoor environments.  
- **Near‑add requirement (bifocals)** – may create a “double‑vision” effect when scanning for obstacles, potentially delaying reaction time in crowded spaces.  
- **No diplopia, full motility** – suggests that mis‑alignment or convergence issues are unlikely contributors to functional difficulties.  
- **Preserved central acuity** – central visual function is adequate, but the hemianopia remains a significant obstacle to safe mobility and daily task performance.

3. **Functional Impact**  

**Mobility and Falls**  
- Difficulty detecting left‑handed doorways and obstacles can lead to collisions and falls, as recently observed.  
- Limited left‑field vision likely increases the need for systematic scanning when turning or navigating narrow passages.  
- Reduced contrast sensitivity may impair detection of low‑contrast steps, handrails, or uneven flooring.

**ADLs/IADLs**  
- Reading text may be slowed by left‑field loss and the need to use a bifocal add.  
- Shopping or cooking in a cluttered kitchen could be hindered by missed peripheral cues.  
- Medication identification may be affected if labels are placed on the left side of the bottle.

**Participation / Confidence / Roles**  
- Uncertainty about safe navigation in crowded indoor settings may reduce willingness to attend social or community activities.  
- Fear of falling could diminish confidence during mobility tasks, affecting overall independence.

**Environmental Interaction**  
- Variable lighting and contrast in indoor spaces may exacerbate field‑loss difficulties.  
- Cluttered pathways, inconsistent furniture placement, or poorly defined handrail edges may increase collision risk.

4. **Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**

**Mobility Strategies**  
- *Assess* the client’s current use of scanning techniques during turning, doorway negotiation, and passage through narrow aisles.  
- *Consider* referral to an Orientation & Mobility specialist if systematic scanning is ineffective or if the client struggles with left‑handed movements.  
- *Explore* the use of a white cane for active boundary detection in cluttered or low‑contrast indoor environments, after confirming suitability and providing training.

**Environmental Modifications / Home Environment**  
- *Assess* overall lighting levels, glare, and contrast throughout the home; recommend high‑contrast pathways (e.g., colored tape or contrasting floor mats) to delineate left‑handed edges.  
- *Recommend* consistent furniture placement and minimal clutter along common routes to reduce collision points.  
- *Advise* on securing loose rugs or ensuring that rugs have non‑slip backing to prevent tripping, especially where left‑side hazards may be missed.

**Task Adaptations**  
- *Assess* the client’s reading strategy and whether he consistently scans to the left before reading.  
- *Recommend* the placement of key visual cues (e.g., bright labels, high‑contrast markers) on the right side of items to facilitate identification.  
- *Consider* the use of a magnifying glass or screen reader for tasks that involve fine visual discrimination, in coordination with low‑vision services if needed.

**Assistive Technology / Non‑Visual Strategies**  
- *Explore* the suitability of a left‑handed “low‑vision identification badge” (e.g., an audible label system) for medication management.  
- *Assess* the feasibility of integrating audio cues into the environment (e.g., motion‑activated lights that announce door openings) to compensate for left‑field loss.  
- *Discuss* the possibility of a smartphone app that audibly announces when the user is approaching a left‑handed doorway or obstacle, contingent on the client’s comfort with technology.

**Education and Support**  
- *Educate* the client and any informal carers on the importance of maintaining clear, predictable pathways, particularly on the left side of the home.  
- *Encourage* the use of a left‑handed side‑specific safety cue (e.g., a colored stripe) on the wall or floor to remind the client to look left when navigating.  
- *Inform* the multidisciplinary team and GP of the hemianopic field loss and its functional implications to support ongoing medical management.

**Clinical Follow‑up / Communication**  
- *Plan* a re‑evaluation of mobility and field‑related safety strategies in 6 months or sooner if new visual symptoms arise or functional status changes.  
- *Maintain* communication with the low‑vision service for potential future intervention if the client’s visual field continues to impact daily activities.

5. **Falls Risk Level**  
**High** – The combination of a permanent left‑hemispheric field loss, a recent fall due to missed left doorway, and comorbid stroke and anticoagulation therapy places the client at high risk for visual‑related falls.

6. **Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings**  
- **Safety‑critical mobility support** – systematic left‑hand scanning, potential cane use, and Orientation & Mobility referral.  
- **Environmental cues and contrast enhancement** – consistent, high‑contrast pathways and clutter reduction to aid left‑field navigation.  
- **Task‑specific adaptations** – right‑side placement of key visual cues, training in scanning for reading and object identification.  
- **Education for client and carers** – focus on predictable pathways and use of left‑hand side cues to mitigate collision risk.

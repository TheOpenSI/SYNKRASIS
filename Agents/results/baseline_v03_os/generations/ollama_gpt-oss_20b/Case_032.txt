==============================
Case 032
==============================


1. Patient Context  
- 80‑year‑old male living with his wife in a community setting.  
- Recurrent outdoor falls (2 in last 4 months) after onset of binocular horizontal diplopia.  
- Left sixth‑nerve palsy (microvascular) causing loss of functional stereopsis and impaired depth perception.  
- Uses bifocals full‑time; currently no driver licence and avoids walking outside alone.  
- Medical comorbidities: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia; on metformin, antihypertensive, statin.  

2. Key Vision Issues  
- **Horizontal binocular diplopia at distance** (≥18 ° ST in primary gaze, worse in left gaze) → impaired depth perception and kerb height judgement.  
- **Reduced functional stereopsis** (manifest deviation, no measurable stereo) → difficulty judging object distance and surface changes.  
- **Contrast sensitivity mildly reduced** (1.5 log units @ 1 m) → challenges in low‑contrast environments such as uneven pavements or dim lighting.  
- **Use of bifocals full‑time** for distance and near tasks → potential mis‑alignment of correction during ambulation (e.g., “safety‑lens” or single‑vision distance lenses advised).  

3. Functional Impact  

*Mobility and Falls*  
- Difficulty judging kerb height and surface changes → increased risk of tripping or falling on uneven footpaths.  
- Need to close one eye when crossing car parks suggests unreliable visual input during navigation.  
- Unsteady when stepping off kerbs, likely due to misjudgement of depth and loss of stereoscopic cues.  

*ADLs/IADLs*  
- **Outdoor walking**: impaired depth perception may affect safe route selection and hazard avoidance.  
- **Self‑care in unfamiliar environments** (e.g., home entry, stairs) may be compromised if kerb or step height is misjudged.  

*Participation/Confidence*  
- Reduced confidence in walking outside alone; likely leads to social isolation or decreased community engagement.  

*Environmental Interaction*  
- Likely increased risk in low‑contrast, uneven, or poorly lit outdoor settings (e.g., uneven footpaths, poorly maintained kerbs).  

4. Vision‑Informed OT Considerations  

**Assessment Before Prescribing**  
- *Mobility*: Assess the patient’s current walking speed, stride variability, and confidence on uneven surfaces with and without single‑vision distance correction.  
- *Environment*: Conduct a home and neighbourhood walk‑through focusing on kerb height, step changes, surface texture, and lighting levels.  
- *Functional Tasks*: Observe and record the patient’s gait pattern and ability to negotiate typical outdoor obstacles (e.g., curbs, curb cuts, raised walkways).  

**Self‑Care**  
- Re‑educate on using single‑vision distance lenses during ambulation; explore the practicality of removing bifocals for walking, or using a “safety” lens (single‑vision distance) with bifocals for near tasks.  

**Environmental Modifications / Home Environment**  
- *Kerb & Step Modification*: Recommend installing low, smooth curb‑cuts where possible, or use of portable step‑lift devices for steep kerbs.  
- *Surface Consistency*: Suggest smoothing uneven footpath areas or using raised “path markers” that are visible in low‑contrast conditions.  

**Lighting and Contrast**  
- Evaluate outdoor lighting (street lights, parking lot lighting).  
- Where feasible, recommend high‑contrast markers (e.g., painted curbs, contrasting sidewalk edges) to aid depth perception in low‑contrast settings.  

**Mobility Strategies**  
- Encourage the use of a cane or walker with a rigid shaft to aid proprioceptive feedback and reduce reliance on visual cues when negotiating kerbs or uneven ground.  
- Discuss safe walking techniques (e.g., “step‑forward” strategy with a “look‑ahead” component) and the importance of maintaining a steady gait.  

**Assistive Technology / Non‑Visual Compensation**  
- *Cane/Walker*: Assess suitability of a cane (or dual‑hand cane) for stability and environmental scanning.  
- *Auditory Feedback Devices*: Explore devices that provide spatial cues (e.g., ultrasonic step‑detecting devices) if visual cues remain unreliable.  

**Support, Carers and Formal Services**  
- Educate wife on maintaining predictable pathways (e.g., clearing clutter, marking step edges).  
- Discuss the possibility of a structured O&M referral for specialised mobility training if persistent depth‑judgement deficits remain after correction changes.  

**Participation & Community Access**  
- Review community walking routes that are well‑lit, flat, and have accessible curb cuts.  
- Explore transportation alternatives (e.g., rideshare, community bus) given cessation of driving.  

**Driving / Transport**  
- Document current driving cessation due to diplopia.  
- Highlight that the patient may need alternative transport solutions to maintain independence and social participation.  

**Clinical Follow‑Up / Communication**  
- Coordinate with GP on systemic work‑up for microvascular disease; maintain a communication loop for any changes in neurological status that could affect vision or mobility.  

5. Falls Risk Level  
**High** – The combination of recurrent outdoor falls, significant horizontal diplopia at distance, loss of stereopsis, and the patient’s own strategy of closing one eye when walking signals a substantial vision‑related fall risk that must be addressed promptly.

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations  
- **Assess and modify** walking strategies and home environment to address depth‑perception deficits and kerb negotiation.  
- **Re‑educate** on appropriate use of single‑vision distance correction during ambulation versus bifocals for near tasks.  
- **Introduce** assistive mobility aids (cane or walker) and consider low‑contrast environmental cues.  
- **Provide** carer education on maintaining clear pathways and support for alternative transport to preserve community participation.  

These priorities derive directly from the documented visual findings, the patient’s fall history, and the contextual factors outlined in the optometry report.

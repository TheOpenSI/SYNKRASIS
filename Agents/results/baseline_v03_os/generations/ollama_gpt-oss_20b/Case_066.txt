==============================
Case 066
==============================


1. **Patient Context**  
   - 71‑year‑old woman, active in the community, walks daily.  
   - Drives only during daylight hours; avoids late‑day driving when symptoms worsen.  
   - Lives independently; no formal home‑care services reported.  
   - Primary visual impairment: variable late‑day blur and reduced contrast sensitivity secondary to ocular surface disease related to glaucoma medication.

2. **Key Vision Issues**  
   - Variable visual clarity in the late afternoon/evening, accompanied by stinging, watering and tear film instability.  
   - Reduced contrast sensitivity (1.35 log units) that limits detection of low‑contrast environmental features.  
   - Uncertainty when negotiating outdoor kerbs and steps late in the day, though no falls have occurred.  
   - Distance and near visual acuity are reasonably preserved (6/9–6/12; N6–N8) and no field loss or diplopia.

3. **Functional Impact**  

   **Mobility and Falls**  
   - Late‑day hesitation when approaching kerbs or steps may increase risk of tripping or misstep.  
   - Variable visual clarity could affect gait rhythm and balance during evening or dusk walking.  
   - No falls reported, but the functional uncertainty suggests a moderate risk that may become manifest in more complex environments.

   **ADLs/IADLs**  
   - Outdoor walking is limited to times of better visual clarity.  
   - Potential difficulty in identifying parking lot markings, curb edges, or uneven surfaces in low‑contrast settings.  
   - Near‑task activities (reading, medication management) are likely unaffected.

   **Participation / Confidence / Roles**  
   - Driving restrictions and evening caution may reduce social participation and independence.  
   - No evidence of reduced confidence beyond the described hesitation.

   **Environmental Interaction**  
   - Reduced contrast sensitivity and intermittent blur may impair detection of shadows, glare from late‑day sun, and subtle changes in floor or curb height.  
   - Evening illumination and transition to lower light levels could exacerbate visual fluctuation.

4. **Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**  

   **Mobility Strategies & O&M**  
   - *Assess* gait and step negotiation under variable visual conditions (e.g., after lubricants, during evening).  
   - Consider referral for Orientation & Mobility assessment if functional evaluation indicates difficulty maintaining a straight path or safe step clearance.  
   - Educate on consistent walking patterns (e.g., steady pace, use of familiar landmarks) and the importance of early tactile cueing (e.g., using a cane or walking stick for step edges when vision is uncertain).  

   **Environmental Modifications / Home Environment**  
   - *Assess* step edges, curb visibility, and floor surface changes in the home and common outdoor paths.  
   - Recommend high‑contrast, tactile markers on steps and kerbs (e.g., contrasting paint, textured striping).  
   - Ensure walkways are free of clutter and that doors/entrances are clearly visible.

   **Lighting and Contrast**  
   - *Assess* ambient and task lighting at times of reduced visual clarity; ensure even illumination to minimise glare and shadows.  
   - Consider installing brighter or adjustable outdoor lights near steps and entrance areas to improve visibility in the evening.  

   **Task Adaptations**  
   - Encourage use of lubricants before planned evening walks to stabilize tear film.  
   - Plan outdoor activities during times of optimal visual clarity; if late‑day walks are necessary, schedule them with a companion or at locations with better lighting and visible markers.  

   **Assistive Technology / Non‑visual Strategies**  
   - Evaluate the potential benefit of a low‑contrast, high‑visibility walking aid (e.g., a white cane with tactile tips).  
   - Teach the use of auditory cues (e.g., hearing a nearby traffic sound) to supplement visual information when approaching curb edges.  

   **Education and Support**  
   - Provide guidance on consistent use of prescribed lubricants and on the timing of glaucoma drops to minimise ocular surface irritation.  
   - Discuss strategies for maintaining a predictable environment (consistent placement of outdoor paths, signage) to support visual scanning.  
   - If a carer or friend is available, involve them in teaching and reinforcing safe walking practices and in monitoring symptom fluctuations.  

   **Clinical Follow‑up / Communication**  
   - Coordinate with the optometrist and ophthalmologist to monitor ocular surface status and visual function, ensuring any change in medication or symptom severity is communicated to the OT for timely adjustment of recommendations.  

   **Support, Carers, Formal Services**  
   - If late‑day walking becomes risky, explore community resources (e.g., senior‑center walking groups, volunteer escort services) to maintain independence.  

   **Driving / Transport**  
   - Confirm the patient’s decision to limit driving to daylight; discuss alternative transport options (public transport, rideshare, family assistance) for evening needs.  

5. **Falls Risk Level**  
   - **Moderate** – Variable late‑day visual clarity, reduced contrast sensitivity, and kerb/step uncertainty increase the risk of tripping or misstep during evening or dusk activity, despite no falls yet.

6. **Summary of Key OT Considerations**  
   - Ensure safe outdoor mobility by assessing and improving step/kerb visibility, lighting, and contrast, and by evaluating the need for an O&M referral.  
   - Teach and reinforce consistent use of lubricants and drop timing to reduce tear film instability before evening activities.  
   - Address driving restrictions and plan alternative transport to support independence.  
   - Monitor visual fluctuations and adjust task adaptations accordingly, coordinating closely with eye‑care professionals.

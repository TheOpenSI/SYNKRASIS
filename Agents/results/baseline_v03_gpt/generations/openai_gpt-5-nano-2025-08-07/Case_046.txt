1. Patient Context
- 74-year-old man in community optometry care following GP referral.
- Primary vision diagnosis: Superior branch retinal vein occlusion with macular edema OS, causing central vision loss OS.
- Other factors: Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, atrial fibrillation; on antihypertensives and anticoagulation.
- Functional history: Gradual central vision decline OS over ~2 months; trouble reading small print and price labels; single low-kerb stumble while visually distracted by blurred vision; right eye maintained better vision.
- Visual status: Left eye OS central impairment with macular edema; right eye OD relatively preserved. Uses separate distance and near spectacles. Drives locally but with reduced driving since left eye worsened.
- Prior ocular treatment: None reported. Ophthalmology referral planned for edema management.

2. Key Vision Issues
- Central vision loss OS from BRVO with macular edema, with reduced distance acuity OS (presenting 6/30, best correction 6/24) and impaired near function (habitual N14 OU; N10 OU with add at 40 cm). Central scotoma and limitation in central fixation OS.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity (1.20 log units at 1 m) and borderline/inconclusive Ishihara colour screening OS (8/14), likely impacted by macular disease.
- Binocular function: Orthophoria at distance; near 4 prism diopters exotropia; stereoacuity severely limited (80” best corrected), limited by central vision loss.
- Mobility risk factors: Central vision loss with impaired reading and central fixation may affect hazard detection during ambulation; one kerb misjudgement previously noted.
- Functional implications for daily tasks: Reading small print and price labels impaired; near tasks and fine detail-dependent activities (e.g., reading labels on medications or products) may be challenging; driving participation reduced but not ceased.

3. Functional Impact
- Mobility and Falls
  - Central vision loss OS and kerb-related stumble suggest potential risk with curb edges, uneven surfaces, and low-contrast environmental features, especially in unfamiliar or cluttered environments.
  - Contrast sensitivity reduction may hinder edge/obstacle detection and safe navigation, particularly in transitional lighting or shadowed areas.
- ADLs / IADLs
  - Reading printed material, price labels, and small print (e.g., medication labels, instructions) is likely to be challenging; near-vision tasks improved with magnification/add, but overall performance may still be variable.
  - Shopping and financial tasks (identifying prices, labels) may require adaptation.
  - Medication management tasks (reading labels, opening containers when labels are small/low contrast) may need adaptation.
- Participation / Confidence / Roles
  - Driving reduced; activity participation may shift toward local tasks or reliance on others for tasks requiring fine central vision or complex reading.
  - Confidence in navigating public spaces and managing near tasks may be affected; potential risk of avoidance of some activities or environments.
- Environmental Interaction
  - Lighting and contrast in home and community environments will influence safety and task ease (glare control, consistent lighting, high-contrast markings for steps/edges).

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

A. ASSESS BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- Assess tasks that rely on central vision and high-detail reading (e.g., reading medication labels, price tags, small print), near-vision function at typical task distances, and mobility safety in real environments (home, street kerbs, steps).
- Determine priority activities for the patient (e.g., reading labels, managing medications, shopping) and any immediate safety concerns.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT AND MODIFICATION
- Evaluate lighting levels, glare, and contrast in key task areas (home entry, kitchen, reading areas). Consider high-contrast, non-glare lighting and task lighting positioned to optimize near tasks.
- Assess clutter and path layout around commonly used routes (home, stairways, doorway transitions) and propose simple, feasible environmental adjustments (clear walkways, consistent furniture placement, high-contrast edge marking for steps).
- Encourage predictable environments to reduce misreads and missteps (consistent item placement, large-print labels where feasible).

C. LIGHTING AND CONTRAST
- Recommend lighting strategies tailored to functional tasks:
  - Even, glare-controlled ambient lighting with adjustable task lighting for near work.
  - High-contrast cues for important edges (e.g., stair edges, door thresholds) without creating glare or distracting shadows.
- Avoid assuming “brighter is always better”; tailor to the patient’s comfort and functional needs.

D. MOBILITY AND ORIENTATION & MOBILITY (O&M)
- Given central vision loss OS and a kerb-related misstep, consider a referral to Orientation & Mobility (O&M) for a formal mobility assessment if mobility safety or community travel is a priority or at risk.
- O&M input should focus on safe environmental strategies and strategies for edge/obstacle detection in real-world settings rather than prescribing specific mobility techniques here.

E. VISUAL + NON-VISUAL COMPENSATION / ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY
- Non-visual and low-vision supports for near tasks:
  - Large-print labels, high-contrast packaging, and tactile identification where feasible.
  - Text-to-speech or audio instructions for reading long-form materials and forms.
  - Magnification options and device accessibility (smartphone, tablet, or computer) with training in use of accessibility features.
- Assess practicality and training needs for these technologies in the patient’s daily routines.

F. TASK ADAPTATION
- Medication management: assess how the patient currently identifies medications and labels; consider a system using large-print labels, color/contrast cues, or pill organizers with distinct compartments.
- Reading-dependent tasks (e.g., price-label identification): explore use of larger print shopping lists, magnified receipts, or audio price lookups if available.
- Shopping and cooking: consider planning strategies that reduce near-vision demands and leverage non-visual cues (tactile cues, consistent labeling).

G. SUPPORT, CARERS AND FORMAL SERVICES
- If significant vision impairment and safety concerns persist, discuss support needs with carers or family (education on environmental safety, consistent routines, and assistance with tasks requiring central vision).
- Consider whether formal support or low-vision services should be involved to support ongoing adaptation and access to resources.

H. PARTICIPATION AND COMMUNITY ACCESS
- Acknowledge reduced driving and explore alternatives to maintain independence and community participation (local transport services, community programs, safe walking routes with appropriate environmental adaptations).
- Discuss strategies to minimize social isolation risk while maintaining safety.

I. DRIVING / LICENSING / TRANSPORT
- Current information: patient drives locally but driving has decreased since left-eye impairment worsened.
- OT role: document functional impact and support planning for alternative transport and safety with public or supported transport; avoid making licensing or driving fitness judgments.

J. EYEWEAR FUNCTION
- Vision management remains optometry-led. OT considerations focus on how current spectacles (distance and near) support or limit functional tasks and whether habitual use is optimized for safety in different environments (e.g., transitions from outdoor to indoor lighting).

K. LOW VISION / SERVICE INPUT
- Acknowledge that low-vision input may be part of ongoing care; coordinate with the ophthalmology/low-vision services as appropriate without duplicating management.

L. BEHAVIOURAL ADAPTATION
- Consider routines or habit changes to support consistent use of preferred aids and clothing/labeling strategies that reduce reliance on uncertain central vision (e.g., keep frequently used items in stable, high-contrast locations).

M. FLUCTUATING VISION
- No explicit fluctuation reported; monitor for any symptom variability (e.g., worsening edema) that could change functional risk and task timing.

N. FEASIBILITY
- Propose modifications and assistive strategies that are realistic within the home and community context (consider cost, practicality, and patient goals).

O. PRIORITISATION
- Safety-critical concerns include mobility hazard risk around steps/kerbs, and safe management of near tasks (medication/shopping) given central vision loss. Prioritize assessment of near-task reading ability, home environment lighting/contrast, and mobility safety; consider O&M referral if community mobility risk is evident.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate falls risk due to central vision loss OS impacting reading and mobility with a single kerb-related incident and reduced contrast sensitivity. Vision contributes to risk but is not the sole factor.

- Mobility safety around kerbs, steps, and uneven surfaces may be compromised by central scotoma and reduced contrast sensitivity; O&M referral could be considered if mobility risk is confirmed as a priority.
- Environmental and task adaptations (lighting, glare control, contrast, consistent layout, high-contrast markings) can support safer functioning without replacing ophthalmology care.

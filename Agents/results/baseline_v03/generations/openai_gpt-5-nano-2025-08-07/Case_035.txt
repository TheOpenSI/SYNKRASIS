Case 035

1. Patient Context

- 67-year-old male in a shared-care glaucoma review in community optometry.
- Primary diagnosis: primary open-angle glaucoma OU with progression; moderate peripheral field/spatial awareness loss phenotype.
- History: glaucoma-treated for ~4 years (latanoprost OU nocte), generally compliant; progression over the past 9 months with reduced confidence negotiating low furniture and difficulty noticing darker floor-level hazards; near misses reported in dim hallways. Medication management with glaucoma therapy under ophthalmology; no central blur, diplopia, or sudden change reported. Active and attends gym; ophthalmology follow-up due in a few months.
- Vision-related findings (documented): Distance VA habitually 6/9 OD, OS, OU = 6/7.5 with distance spectacles; Best-corrected distance VA 6/7.5 OU. Near VA habitual N8 OU at 40 cm; with add best corrected N5 OU. Contrast sensitivity 1.35 log units at 1 m (reduced). Binocular alignment orthophoria distance; near 4 prism diopters exotropia. Stereo 80" (reduced). Full motility; no diplopia; pupils equal/reactive. IOP approx. 17–18 mmHg. Ocular health shows mild conjunctival hyperaemia from topical therapy; early nuclear sclerosis OU; optic nerve with inferior rim thinning OU and focal notch OS; macular pigment change OU. OCT confirms inferior RNFL thinning OD, progressive; inferior-temporal thinning OS; progressive; VF reliability good with documented glaucomatous defects OU (OD MD −7.0 dB; OS MD −8.0 dB). Glaucoma progression noted; VFI around 86–84%.
- Functional/occupational context: Reports difficulty detecting floor-level hazards in dim lighting; several near-misses in dim hallways; currently uses distance spectacles for walking and readers for near tasks.
- Driving: Drives daytime and occasional dusk trips.
- Other health/context: Hypertension, obstructive sleep apnoea.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Peripheral field loss with progressive inferior/ arcuate defects (documented). Functional relevance: reduced awareness of floor-level hazards and low-lying obstacles, especially in dim lighting; risk during ambulation at home and in dim corridors.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson ~1.35 at 1 m). Functional relevance: difficulty detecting low-contrast hazards on the floor or in dim environments; may contribute to near-miss events and slower obstacle detection.
- Central acuity relatively preserved, but overall functional vision limited by field loss. Functional relevance: just enough central clarity to read or recognize but with impaired spatial awareness in periphery.
- Depth perception/stereo reduced (80"); near exophoria (4 XP). Functional relevance: may affect judging steps, drop-offs, and distances during outdoor or indoor ambulation, particularly when stepping around furniture or low obstacles.
- Visual function fluctuations not reported; overall stability uncertain due to glaucoma progression. Functional relevance: consider variability with lighting or fatigue or time of day in OT planning.
- Driving: current driving primarily daytime with some dusk driving; functional implications of field loss and low-contrast sensitivity on driving safety, especially in low-light settings. Requires careful consideration in OT planning (education/support, not licensing decisions).
  
3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- At home and in dim hallways, may have difficulty detecting floor-level hazards (e.g., thresholds, low furniture edges, clutter) leading to increased near-miss risk.
- Inferior/ arcuate field loss and reduced stereo may contribute to misjudging step height or distance when navigating around obstacles; depth perception could be less reliable in uneven or cluttered environments.
- Lighting quality and contrast of flooring/obstacles are critical; glare or shadows could further obscure hazards.

ADLs/IADLs
- Reading/near tasks with current near vision is maintained with readers, but reduced contrast sensitivity could affect reading labels or forms, especially in low light or with low-contrast print.
- Medication management and household tasks could be impacted when tasks occur in dim areas or involve moving around low-contrast surfaces.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Reported reduced confidence negotiating low furniture and floor hazards; may lead to activity modification or avoidance of dimly lit/home environments, with potential implications for independence and routine.

Environmental Interaction
- Lighting and clutter in home environment are key modifiable factors; floor clutter and low-level hazards are particularly relevant given field loss and near-miss history.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate falls risk related to peripheral field loss, reduced contrast sensitivity, and history of near-misses in dim hallways.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

A. ASSESS BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- Assess home environment and habitual routes (hallways, stairs, transitions between rooms) under typical lighting conditions (including dim/low-light periods) to identify specific hazard points.
- Determine current use/pattern of eyewear during ambulation (distance glasses for walking vs near glasses) and assess consistency of spectacle use across activities.
- Evaluate whether vision-related symptoms (e.g., fatigue, time of day) alter function; consider function during symptomatic vs non-symptomatic times if feasible.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT AND MODIFICATION
- Modify home environment to reduce hazard exposure:
  - Improve general and task lighting in corridors, stairs, and frequently used rooms; ensure even illumination with minimal glare and shadows.
  - Remove floor clutter and secure loose rugs; ensure clear walkways with unobstructed paths to essential areas (e.g., doorway to kitchen, bathroom, bedroom).
  - Use high-contrast, non-slip flooring transitions at floor edges and around furniture; consider high-contrast edge markings on steps/thresholds.
  - Arrange furniture to create wide, consistent walking lanes; ensure predictable routes between commonly used spaces.
- Consider installation of tactile or auditory cues in high-risk areas (e.g., kitchen thresholds, bathroom thresholds) if appropriate and feasible.

C. LIGHTING AND CONTRAST
- Prioritize even, glare-controlled lighting with minimal shadowing in key movement areas (hallways, stairways, approach to furniture).
- Use task lighting that provides adequate illumination for floor-level hazard detection without creating competing glare; consider color temperature that reduces contrast confusion.

D. MOBILITY AND ORIENTATION & MOBILITY (O&M)
- Refer to Orientation & Mobility for a formal mobility assessment and individualized strategies; focus on:
  - Scanning strategies and safe navigation in familiar/unfamiliar environments.
  - Route planning and safe movement in dim lighting or cluttered spaces.
  - Non-visual mobility strategies and, if appropriate, human-guide considerations or mobility aids planning.
- Do not prescribe a fixed mobility routine; adapt planning to the individual’s environment and preferences.

E. VISUAL + NON-VISUAL COMPENSATION / ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY
- Consider non-visual compensation and environmental supports:
  - Consistent placement and organization of frequently used items to reduce search time.
  - Large-print labels or high-contrast signage for key home areas; audible/guided navigation cues if needed.
  - Smartphone or tablet accessibility features (larger text, high-contrast mode) for medication or form-related tasks if used.
- Eyewear considerations:
  - Encourage consistent use of distance spectacles for walking to maximize peripheral awareness, while ensuring transitions between tasks do not introduce conflicting visual inputs.
  - If near tasks become challenging in dim light, discuss with eye-care provider whether task lighting or reading aids may be helpful, without mandating a device prescription.

F. TASK ADAPTATION
- Analyze specific tasks that pose risk (e.g., moving around low furniture, stepping over thresholds) and adapt task setup accordingly (clear paths, step marks, seating arrangements that reduce the need to navigate around obstacles).

G. SUPPORT, CARERS AND FORMAL SERVICES
- Assess need for caregiver or household member support in navigating home spaces or during times of decreased visibility (e.g., dusk or fatigue).
- Provide education to household members on consistent environmental organization and hazard reduction strategies.
- Coordinate with ophthalmology and low-vision services as appropriate to align visual function supports with OT goals.

H. PARTICIPATION AND COMMUNITY ACCESS
- Proactively plan for safe community participation with vision loss, including strategies for navigating dimly lit public spaces and avoiding environments with poor contrast.
- Discuss transportation options and potential limitations related to driving at dusk or in low-contrast conditions; plan for safer transportation alternatives if needed while preserving independence where possible.

I. DRIVING / LICENSING / TRANSPORT RULE — CRITICAL
- Given daytime driving with occasional dusk trips and known field loss, address driving-related risks through education and safety planning:
  - Discuss potential impact of peripheral field loss and reduced contrast sensitivity on road-hazard detection, especially in low-light conditions.
  - Explore alternatives to maintain community access (public transport, ride-sharing, planned travel times when visibility is better) and discuss with ophthalmology if driving recommendations or restrictions are being considered.
  - Do not determine driving fitness; coordinate with eye-care providers for any clinical driving clearance.

J. EYEWEAR FUNCTION RULE
- Ensure walking in various environments uses the most appropriate glasses for safety:
  - For walking/ambulation, confirm consistent use of distance spectacles to support spatial awareness; avoid switching to readers during ambulation if it reduces distance clarity.
- If the patient reports difficulties transitioning between glasses or tasks, consider a low-vision/OT liaison to discuss practical strategies rather than eyewear changes.

K. LOW VISION / LOW-VISION SERVICE RULE
- If a low-vision service is planned or available, coordinate care and avoid duplicating services; OT focus remains on interim task setup and safety, non-visual strategies, and environmental modifications.

L. BEHAVIOURAL ADAPTATION RULE
- If evidence of inconsistent use of eyewear or routines emerges, implement practical habit strategies (consistent storage, cueing, routine linking) to improve safety.

M. FLUCTUATING VISION RULE
- Not currently described; if symptoms fluctuate (fatigue, diurnal variation), consider assessing function at differing times and advising task timing adjustments where feasible.

N. FEASIBILITY RULE
- All recommendations should be realistic within the patient’s home and daily routine, considering mobility, social supports, cost, and access to services.

O. PRIORITISATION
- Priority 1: Safety in dim/low-contrast environments; home environment lighting and clutter reduction; floor-level hazard visibility.
- Priority 2: Mobility/O&M assessment to tailor scanning/navigation strategies and confirm safe mobility planning.
- Priority 3: Driving/transit planning and education; coordinate with ophthalmology for any clinical implications.
- Priority 4: Visual compensation strategies (non-visual supports, task lighting, consistent eyewear use) and supports for participation and confidence.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate falls risk related to peripheral field loss, reduced contrast, and near-miss history in dim hallways. Vision constraints interact with environmental factors and daytime/dusk mobility situations.

5. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- The patient has progressive peripheral field loss with inferior arcuate defects and reduced depth perception, resulting in compromised obstacle detection for floor-level hazards, especially in dim lighting.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity and near stereo vision contribute to difficulty identifying low-contrast floor hazards and accurately judging distances around low obstacles.
- Functional priority is improving safe ambulation at home and in dim environments through environmental modifications, lighting adjustments, and safe movement strategies; driving risk at dusk should be addressed through education and planning rather than licensing decisions.
- OT assessment should focus on home environment assessment and modification, mobility/O&M referral, and practical strategies for non-visual compensation and task adaptation to preserve safety and participation.

Final notes for case-handling
- This summary does not replace optometry management. It translates vision findings into OT-relevant functional implications and identifies assessment and intervention considerations that are appropriate for an OT handover.
- If additional case-specific information emerges (e.g., home layout details, available supports, or explicit priorities from the patient), integrate those into the OT plan while maintaining the boundary that OT recommendations remain contingent on clinical judgment and scope.

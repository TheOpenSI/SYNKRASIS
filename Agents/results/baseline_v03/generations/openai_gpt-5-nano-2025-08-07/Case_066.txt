Case 066

1. Patient Context
- Age: 71 years; female
- Care setting: community optometry practice
- Falls pathway / referral context: routine examination where variable visual clarity and evening mobility hesitation were identified during history
- Vision/functional context: ocular surface disease with fluctuating late-day visual blur; known glaucoma on multi-drop therapy; polypharmacy
- Spectacle use: single-vision distance spectacles for walking; readers for near
- Driving: daytime driving only; avoids late-day driving when irritated
- Key ocular history: long-standing glaucoma; tear film instability with topical therapy-related irritation; mild meibomian dysfunction; early nuclear sclerosis
- Current functional note: remains active with daily walking; late-day blur specifically affects confidence navigating kerbs/steps

2. Key Vision Issues
- Fluctuating/variable vision linked to ocular surface disease from chronic topical glaucoma therapy (likely preservative-related), causing late-day blur and reduced kerb/step visibility; this is the main driver of reduced outdoor mobility confidence.
- Tear film instability with conjunctival hyperaemia and punctate epithelial staining contributing to symptoms that worsen through the day.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson 1.35 log units at 1 m) increasing difficulty detecting step/curb edges and low-contrast obstacles, particularly in late-day lighting.
- Stereo/acuity: distance best-corrected VA modestly reduced (OU 6/7.5–6/9 with best correction); near VA shows reliance on reading glasses (N8 habitual; N6 with add best corrected) indicating persistent near tasks demand and presbyopia.
- Glaucoma history with cupping; full fields on confrontation, no diplopia or new field-type symptoms reported.
- Ocular health: diffuse anterior segment signs consistent with ocular surface disease; early nuclear sclerosis; overall vision relatively preserved but temporally fluctuating.

3. Functional Impact
Mobility and Falls
- Late-day worsening blur and reduced contrast may impair accurate detection of step/kerb edges and obstacles, increasing hesitation and safety risk for outdoor ambulation, particularly on uneven surfaces or in unfamiliar environments.
- No documented falls, but risk may be heightened in poorer lighting or at times of symptomatic fluctuation.

ADLs/IADLs
- Reading outdoor signs or environmental cues may be inconsistently aided by fluctuating vision; near tasks (reading labels at distance outdoors, signage) could be intermittently challenging during symptom peaks.
- Medication management tasks are not explicitly documented as affected, but fluctuating vision could influence reading small print on labels during symptomatic periods.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Evening/late-day mobility hesitation and kerb/step uncertainty may reduce outdoor activity, social participation, and confidence in independent walking in variable conditions.
- Driving largely restricted to daytime; late-day driving avoided, which could limit some community activities or errands if schedules require evening mobility.

Environmental Interaction
- Tear-film–related glare, shadows and variable contrast likely interact with lighting transitions (sunset, dusk) and surface contrasts, complicating edge detection and safe navigation.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations
A. Assessment Before Prescribing
- Assess outdoor mobility safety specifically during symptomatic times (late day) to identify high-risk tasks and routes.
- Explore consistency of eyewear use and whether the current setup (distance glasses for walking, readers for near) remains functionally optimal across environments and lighting.

B. Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- For outdoor routes used in late day, consider environmental supports that enhance edge/step visibility and reduce glare (e.g., well-lit paths, attention to contrast at transitions).
- Advise planning routes with higher-contrast curb/step edges when possible; use predictable, clutter-free pathways.

C. Lighting and Contrast
- Consider how ambient lighting and sun angle affect edge visibility; assess potential glare from low sun or artificial lighting in travel areas and discuss glare-control strategies.

D. Mobility Strategies
- O&M considerations may be appropriate if mobility risk is judged to be clinically significant; strategies could focus on scanning, route planning, and use of non-visual cues during symptomatic periods without prescribing a specific mobility protocol.
- Emphasize safety planning for late-day ambulation and stepping strategies rather than procedural mobility sequences.

E. Task Adaptation
- Explore scheduling outdoor activities earlier in the day when vision is more stable; build in rest periods or avoid visually demanding tasks during peak symptom times.
- Consider consistent routine for eye-care that may reduce fluctuations (e.g., lubricants before outdoor walking) and alignment with ophthalmology recommendations.

F. Assistive Technology / Non-Visual Strategies
- Non-visual compensations may support safety during symptomatic periods: tactile cues at thresholds, consistent pacing, deliberate scanning strategies, and use of environmental cues.
- Eye-care collaboration to minimize preservative exposure or switch to preservative-free formulations may reduce symptom burden; if feasible, plan around cost/availability with the patient and ophthalmology.

G. Education and Support
- Educate about symptom timing and safety considerations for late-day activity; reinforce use of lubricants and preservative-free drops as advised by the ophthalmologist.
- Provide guidance on immediate safety steps (e.g., pause and blink, resume ambulation after symptoms subside) and encourage avoidance of high-risk outdoor tasks when irritated.

H. Clinical Follow-Up / Communication
- Coordinate with the optometry ophthalmology team regarding medication changes or preservative-free alternatives; document any functional changes at follow-up in 6 weeks with emphasis on falls risk and mobility safety.
- Continue to monitor IOP control and ocular surface status; document any functional improvements or emerging risks.

I. Orientation & Mobility (O&M)
- Given material impact on safe community mobility, consider an O&M referral if mobility risk remains after addressing ocular surface symptoms and environmental supports; avoid initiating specialist techniques without confirming need and appropriateness.

J. Driving / Transport
- Current status: daytime driving only; avoidance of late-day driving when symptoms are present. OT considerations should support safety planning and transport alternatives during symptomatic periods; do not assess fitness to drive beyond this context.

K. Eyewear Function
- Maintain emphasis on consistent use of current eyewear and ensure alignment of tasks with appropriate distance or near correction; no optometric prescription changes advised by OT.

L. Low Vision / Multidisciplinary Input
- If low-vision input is planned or available via the eye care team, acknowledge and coordinate; OT focus remains on interim functional setup, safe task participation and environmental adaptations.

M. Fluctuating Vision / Context
- Acknowledge the fluctuating nature of vision as a core consideration; assess function at symptomatic and non-symptomatic times and adapt task timing or strategies accordingly.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate
- Rationale: fluctuating late-day vision with tear-film instability and reduced contrast increases difficulty with curb/step detection; no falls to date, but mobility safety is potentially compromised during symptom peaks; glaucoma is stable and no diplopia; overall risk is not currently high but warrants cautious management and targeted assessment.

5. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings
- The principal issue is medication-related ocular surface disease driving late-day, fluctuating blur with reduced contrast, impacting outdoor mobility safety (kerbs/steps) and confidence.
- Environmental and task adaptations should focus on reducing glare/contrast barriers, planning activities for earlier in the day, and ensuring consistent eyewear use while coordinating with the ophthalmology team about preservative-free options.
- Consider O&M input if mobility risk remains prominent after addressing ocular surface symptoms and environmental supports.
- Ensure education, safety planning, and follow-up communication with the eye-care team, with a 6-week reassessment interval to evaluate ocular surface status, IOP, adherence, and functional impact.

Final notes
- This OT summary translates documented optometry findings and the referral context into occupational performance implications, highlighting plausible functional impacts, assessment needs, and environment- and task-focused mitigations without altering the medical plan or prescribing vision care.

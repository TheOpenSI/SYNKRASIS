Case 083

1. Patient Context

- Age and setting: 78-year-old female, community-based shared-care glaucoma follow-up.
- Vision problem: Progressive normal-tension glaucoma OU with inferior peripheral field loss, contributing to reduced spatial awareness and side-collision risk (noted history of clipping doorframes and laundry baskets).
- Visual function snapshot: Distance VA 6/9 OD, 6/12 OS, best-corrected OU 6/7.5 at 6 m; contrast sensitivity reduced (1.35 log units at 1 m); VF shows inferior defects OD and OS with progression OU; stereo 80" (reduced depth perception); near vision functional with readers; no RAPD; mild nuclear sclerosis and conjunctivochalasis OU; OCT and VF show structure–function progression.
- Functional status: Independent at home but cautious in cluttered environments; uses single-vision distance spectacles for walking and separate readers; driving limited to short daytime distances; advised to refrain from driving pending Esterman testing.
- Medical context: Hypertension (on antihypertensives), migraine, Raynaud’s phenomenon.
- Referral context: Falls pathway—increasing side-collision history identified during routine glaucoma follow-up; no recent falls but near-miss collisions with environmental obstacles.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Progressive bilateral inferior peripheral field loss (with OD more affected in nasal/inferior field and OS with inferior/paracentral loss). Functional relevance: diminishes detection of hazards coming from the side and from below, increasing risk of side collisions during ambulation and turning, particularly in hallways or cluttered spaces.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity (1.35 log units at 1 m). Functional relevance: lower contrast makes edge detection and obstacle discernment harder in clutter, dim lighting, or busy environments.
- Reduced binocular depth perception (stereo 80"). Functional relevance: may affect judgment of step or curb edge, distance to door thresholds, and safe timing of steps during transitions or turning.
- Visual field progression confirmed on automated testing (HVF 24-2; MDs around -6 to -7 dB OU; event analysis showing progression OU; VFI progressive). Functional relevance: ongoing risk of unnoticed hazards in daily mobility and community navigation; supports need for compensatory strategies and possible O&M input.
- Ocular comorbidity contributing to vision function: moderate nuclear sclerosis and mild conjunctivochalasis may further reduce contrast and glare tolerance; combined with glaucomatous loss, this can amplify functional impact in real-world environments.
- Driving status: Currently driving short daytime distances; vision loss may impact driving safety; plan to refrain from driving until formal fitness testing (Esterman) is completed.

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- In-home hallway navigation and turning in cluttered spaces may be compromised due to inferior/peripheral field loss and reduced contrast; risk of side collisions with doorframes, furniture, or laundry items.
- Community mobility is likely to be affected by driving restriction in the near term; dependence on alternatives (public/arranged transport) may be needed to maintain independence, especially for appointments or social activities.
- Depth perception reductions may challenge safe stepping and threshold transitions (e.g., step edges, doorway thresholds) in varied lighting.

ADLs/IADLs
- Reading/recognizing items or labels may be indirectly affected by reduced contrast and peripheral field loss, particularly in cluttered or low-contrast environments (not a primary stated deficit but plausible in complex tasks such as grocery labeling, medication packaging in low light).

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Increasing caution in cluttered environments and potential driving restriction may influence confidence in leaving home, shopping, or attending social activities; risk of activity restriction if transportation is limited.
- Ongoing adaptation to bilateral vision changes may require assistance with planning or support for higher-risk activities (e.g., negotiating unfamiliar environments).

Environmental Interaction
- Environmental lighting and clutter are likely contributing factors; suboptimal hallway lighting and edge/contrast cues may compound difficulty detecting hazards.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

A. ASSESS BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- Assess home environment for clutter density, hallway width, furniture placement, doorway visibility, and existing lighting quality (glare, evenness, and contrast at thresholds).
- Assess the real-world impact of right-hemisphere/ inferior-field loss on scanning and turning in common routes (hallways, entry/exit points).
- Assess driving/screened mobility status and transportation needs in the context of pending Esterman testing; discuss interim transport options and safety.
- Consider an Orientation & Mobility (O&M) referral to tailor mobility safety and route planning in the context of peripheral field loss, without assuming specific techniques.
- Consider referral to low-vision services for functional magnification/contrast strategies if non-visual compensations are needed beyond what OT can address.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT AND MODIFICATION
- Recommend environmental modifications focused on functional safety: improve hallway lighting (even illumination, reduce glare, eliminate shadows where feasible), declutter pathways, and use high-contrast edge cues for doorways and furniture to enhance edge detection.
- Encourage predictable layouts and consistent furniture placement to minimize unexpected obstacles in common travel routes.
- Ensure accessible task areas with clear pathways, particularly near entry/exit points and in clutter-prone zones.

C. LIGHTING AND CONTRAST
- Evaluate and optimize lighting to balance glare and contrast; avoid abrupt lighting level changes when moving between spaces; consider task lighting for key navigation areas (e.g., hallway transitions).
- Use high-contrast, non-glare finishes at thresholds and doorway edges to improve edge visibility.

D. MOBILITY AND ORIENTATION & MOBILITY (O&M)
- Given observable spatial awareness loss, pursue an O&M assessment/referral to determine appropriate mobility supports, strategies, and environmental adaptations for safe community and home ambulation.
- The OT role is to coordinate with O&M for mobility safety, route planning, and accommodation needs rather than prescribe specific mobility techniques.

E. VISUAL + NON-VISUAL COMPENSATION / ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY
- Explore non-visual strategies to support safe navigation: consistent item placement, audible/environmental cues, and routine scanning checks before turning; non-vision-based strategies may be used alongside vision-based compensations.
- Consider assistive technology and accessibility features (e.g., smartphones with audio navigation or text-to-speech for labels/receipts) where aligned with goals and feasible.

F. TASK ADAPTATION
- Assess specific tasks that may be affected by field loss (e.g., entering/exiting rooms, turning in corridors, approaching door thresholds) and tailor adaptations rather than assuming broad deficits.
- For example, plan for consistent placement of frequently used items to reduce reliance on hard-to-see sensory cues.

G. EDUCATION AND SUPPORT
- Provide education about safety strategies for neighbors/family (e.g., maintaining clear walkways, consistent lighting, supportive mobility practices).
- Discuss driving cessation implications and support planning, including transportation alternatives, scheduling considerations, and contingency plans for essential appointments.
- Coordinate with family or caregivers as appropriate for vision-related safety reinforcement and background support.

H. CLINICAL FOLLOW-UP / COMMUNICATION
- Ensure timely communication with eye care providers regarding functional safety concerns and progression; document and share vision-related risk implications with the patient’s multidisciplinary team.

I. DRIVING / TRANSPORT (from source-supported information)
- Acknowledge the plan to refrain from driving until formal Esterman testing, and translate into OT-level considerations:
  - Assess how driving cessation or restriction would affect independence and community participation.
  - Plan for transportation alternatives, banking/shopping needs, and appointment scheduling.
  - Consider driver rehabilitation or O&M input for safe alternative mobility planning if appropriate.

J. EYEWEAR FUNCTION
- No recommendations to alter spectacles are included; translate current eyewear use into functional implications:
  - Walks with distance spectacles; potential temporary reliance on readers for near tasks; ensure consistent use in known environments to maximize edge and hazard detection.
  - If mobility safety becomes an ongoing concern, consider whether eyewear use should be reviewed in collaboration with the optometrist, particularly during transitions or in high-contrast environments.

K. LOW VISION / LOW-VISION SERVICE
- Acknowledge that low-vision input may be beneficial but do not duplicate ophthalmology plans; coordinate with the LV service if available to enhance task adaptations, environmental modifications, and accessibility features within OT scope.

L. BEHAVIOURAL ADAPTATION
- If inconsistent use of any aids or routines is encountered, implement simple habit-based strategies (e.g., consistent pathways, cueing, routine checks before turning) to support safe participation, while recognising vision-related limitations.

M. FLUCTUATING VISION
- Vision appears progression-based rather than fluctuating; monitor for any time-of-day or fatigue-related variation that could alter task risk; adjust planning accordingly.

N. FEASIBILITY
- Consider home environment feasibility for modifications (lighting and clutter management) given the patient’s current living situation; balance safety gains with practicality.

O. PRIORITISATION
- Primary priority: safety in ambulation and turning due to progressive inferior field loss and reduced contrast; driving safety and community mobility due to planned cessation; secondary priority: environmental modifications to support safe navigation.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate
  Rationale: Bilateral inferior field loss with progression, reduced contrast sensitivity, and real-world reports of side collisions in cluttered environments increase risk of mobility-related incidents, even though the patient remains independent and without recent falls.

5. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- Prioritize assessment and mitigation of side-collision risk during ambulation due to progressive bilateral inferior field loss and reduced contrast sensitivity; consider O&M input for safety in hallway navigation and turning.
- Address environmental factors (lighting, glare, clutter, contrast at edges) to support safer mobility and edge detection in habitual routes.
- Plan for driving/transport implications with emphasis on safety, independence, and alternative mobility options; coordinate with mobility services and patient goals.
- Explore visual and non-visual compensation strategies (consistent item placement, non-visual cues, task lighting) and consider low-vision referral if appropriate to enhance functional performance.
- Recognize potential modest impact on activities requiring precise depth perception or hazard detection in dynamic environments; tailor assessment to task-specific contexts (e.g., hallway turning, thresholds, and doorways).

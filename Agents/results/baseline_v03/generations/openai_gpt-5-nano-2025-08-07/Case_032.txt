Case 032

1. Patient Context
- Age: 80 years; male
- Setting: community optometry practice; GP referral after recurrent outdoor falls
- Vision-related history: three-month binocular horizontal diplopia, worse at distance and in left gaze; difficulty judging kerb height; two falls on uneven footpaths in past 4 months; closes one eye when crossing car parks; reduced walking outside alone
- Visual function notes: full-field status; left sixth nerve palsy suspected (microvascular etiology) with left-gaze–predominant diplopia and loss of functional stereopsis; distance acuity improved with best refractive correction (OD/OS/OU ~6/9–6/12); near acuity relatively preserved; contrast sensitivity mildly reduced; color vision intact
- Eye health: mild nuclear sclerosis and dermatochalasis; macula with small drusen; no diabetic retinopathy; IOP normal; no RAPD
- Functional status and risks: stopped driving after diplopia; recurrent outdoor mobility limits; recent falls on outdoor surfaces; relies on wife for outdoor companionship; diabetes and vascular risk factors present
- Management plan from optometry: temporary occlusion or Fresnel prism discussed for safer mobility; single-vision distance correction recommended for walking tasks; review in 12 weeks or sooner if new neuro symptoms

2. Key Vision Issues
- Binocular diplopia with left abduction deficit and horizontal misalignment, particularly affecting distance vision and left gaze
- Loss of functional stereopsis due to diplopia, contributing to depth-perception impairment
- Mildly reduced distance acuity with habitual bifocals; near vision largely preserved
- Mild contrast sensitivity reduction
- Visual field grossly full; no RAPD
- Overall, diplopia and impaired depth cues are the primary visual contributors to outdoor mobility risk

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- Outdoor ambulation is compromised by depth perception loss and diplopia, increasing risk of missing curb heights, steps, and uneven surfaces
- Two recent falls on outdoor footpaths and difficulty judging kerbs heighten risk during community mobility; reduced independent outdoor walking and cessation of driving
- May rely more on the partner for navigation and safer routes; safety in unfamiliar environments and at transitions (curbs, street edges) is a concern

ADLs/IADLs
- Indoor/household activities not explicitly described as impaired; primary OT-relevant implications relate to outdoor mobility and safety during shopping, errands, and social outings
- Reading of distance-critical information (e.g., street signs, shop displays) may be affected in outdoor contexts; near tasks appear less affected

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Outdoor participation likely constrained; increased dependence on spouse for accompaniment; potential impact on social participation and community access
- Driving cessation, while protective, reduces independent mobility and may affect activities previously undertaken outside the home

Environmental Interaction
- Outdoor mobility environments (sunlight, glare, shadows, clutter) may exacerbate diplopia and depth-perception challenges; curb edges and step transitions are particularly salient risks

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

A. ASSESS BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- Assess outdoor mobility context and routes that the patient uses with her husband; observe curb negotiation, step-down/step-up transitions, and surface changes in a safe environment or simulated setting
- Confirm tolerance and functional use of single-vision distance correction for walking tasks (as recommended by optometry) and how it affects balance and confidence
- Identify situations with peak diplopia (distance viewing, left gaze) to tailor functional precautions

B. ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT AND MODIFICATION
- Consider environmental factors that may reduce risk during outdoor tasks: even lighting, glare reduction, and high-contrast curb/step edges; assess home entry/egress for lighting and clutter that could compound navigation difficulties
- Advise on predictable walking paths and avoidance of high-risk outdoor routes until mobility is optimized

C. LIGHTING AND CONTRAST
- If assessing home or community access, consider lighting quality and contrast at entryways, curb transitions, and doorway thresholds to support edge detection and obstacle awareness

D. MOBILITY AND ORIENTATION & MOBILITY (O&M) RELEVANCE
- Given material impact on safe community mobility, a referral to Orientation & Mobility (O&M) is appropriate to tailor mobility strategy for diplopia and depth-perception limitations
- O&M input can focus on scanning strategies, contingency planning for obstacle detection, and safe navigation in unfamiliar or busy environments; exact techniques should be individualized by the mobility specialist

E. VISUAL + NON-VISUAL COMPENSATION / ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY
- Explore non-visual compensation approaches (consistent routes, tactile cues, auditory cues, partner support) to supplement limited depth cues
- Non-visual strategies and available aids should be considered in parallel with eye care management; avoid universal reliance on devices without individual assessment
- Eyewear strategy: reinforce that single-vision distance correction is intended for mobility; avoid reliance on bifocals during walking unless cleared by the eye-care team

F. TASK ADAPTATION
- When safe, plan walking activities with routes that minimize complex curb negotiation and heavy crowding
- Consider task ordering (e.g., perform outdoor errands with a familiar partner or in daylight) to reduce risk during suboptimal vision
- If reading distance-related information is needed during outings (signs, taxi numbers), assess whether magnification or larger-print cues could support task performance

G. SUPPORT, CARERS AND FORMAL SERVICES
- Family or carer involvement is important for safe community participation; educate the support person on strategies to facilitate safe mobility (e.g., maintaining predictable routes, signaling when curb edge is near)
- Consider whether formal support or assistive mobility services are needed during the transition period (especially while diplopia may recover)

H. PARTICIPATION AND COMMUNITY ACCESS
- Goal to preserve community participation with safe outdoor access; identify acceptable activities and routes that align with safety and independence
- Address risk of social isolation due to mobility limitations by planning accessible outings with supports

I. DRIVING / LICENSING / TRANSPORT
- Patient has stopped driving; OT role includes supporting transition planning to maintain community access through alternative transport, scheduling, and acceptance of new routines
- Discuss strategies for arranging and practicing safe non-driving mobility options and coordinating transport with the spouse

J. EYEWEAR FUNCTION
- Emphasize temporary use of single-vision distance correction for outdoor mobility to reduce image displacement from bifocals
- If prisms or occlusion are used, coordinate with optometry to ensure safe practice and avoid conflicting visual cues; support the patient and family in understanding how these changes affect daily activities

K. LOW-VISION / LOW-VISION SERVICE
- Not currently presenting as low-vision needing device-based rehabilitation; focus remains on functional adaptation, safety, and environmental/compensation strategies; liaison with low-vision services can be considered if functional goals shift

L. BEHAVIOURAL ADAPTATION
- Acknowledge adaptive behaviors (e.g., eye closure in car parks) as compensatory mechanisms; support gradual adoption of safer, more consistent strategies that enhance safety without excessive restriction

M. FLUCTUATING VISION
- If diplopia or symptoms fluctuate (e.g., due to fatigue or time of day), plan to assess function at symptomatic vs. non-symptomatic times and adjust activity timing and route choices accordingly

N. FEASIBILITY
- Propose feasible OT-relevant strategies given living situation (aging-in-place with spouse), outdoor mobility limitations, and availability of support
- Recommend endorsements for O&M and environmental adaptations that are practical within the home and community contexts

O. PRIORITISATION
- Safety-critical: curb and kerb negotiation, stepping onto/off curbs, uneven surfaces during outdoor mobility
- High relevance to independence: outdoor community access and reliance on a partner for mobility
- Modifiable factors: footwear, lighting/contrast considerations, route planning, and non-visual compensations
- Likely to change management: progression/recovery from microvascular CN VI palsy; re-evaluate after recovery window

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate
- Rationale: recurrent outdoor falls with diplopia and impaired depth perception; risk is primarily linked to a potentially recoverable binocular/movement deficit and age-related factors; not all impairment is fixed, but current functional risk is meaningful and warrants targeted assessment and strategy development

5. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings
- Recurrent outdoor falls are closely linked to binocular depth-perception impairment from recent-onset left CN VI palsy; mobility safety outdoors is the priority
- Requires mobility-focused assessment (likely O&M referral) to tailor safe community navigation and curb/step negotiation with diplopia
- Walking with single-vision distance correction may be safer; OT can support integrating this eyewear strategy into daily routines and practice
- Transportation planning and social participation implications should be addressed (driving cessation, alternative transport, caregiver involvement)
- Environmental and non-visual compensation considerations (lighting, contrast, predictable routes, tactile/auditory cues) should be explored to reduce fall risk during outdoor activity

Notes and next steps for the OT clinician:
- Consider an OT-focused referral to Orientation & Mobility for a structured mobility assessment and education on safety strategies, with emphasis on curb detection, obstacle negotiation, and route selection
- Reinforce the use of single-vision distance correction for walking; coordinate with optometry regarding any prism/occlusion use and timing
- Assess for home and community environment adjustments that support safe mobility (lighting, clutter reduction, high-contrast edge cues)
- Plan for driving cessation implications, including transportation alternatives and caregiver support education
- Monitor changes over the 12-week window; be prepared to adjust OT priorities if recovery from diplopia occurs or if new neurological symptoms emerge

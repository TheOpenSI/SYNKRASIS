Case 032

1. Patient Context

- Age: 80; male; community setting (optometry clinic following GP referral)
- Falls pathway: referred after recurrent outdoor falls; GP noted intermittent diplopia and difficulty judging kerb height
- Vision problem: recent onset binocular horizontal diplopia, worse at distance and left gaze; depth perception dysfunction; left sixth nerve palsy suspected (likely microvascular etiology)
- Functional status: stops driving after diplopia; reduces outdoor walking alone; compensates by closing one eye when crossing car parks
- Vision designations: full-field binocular involvement with left abduction deficit; distance VA best corrected OU 6/12 to 6/9 with single-vision distance correction recommended for walking; near vision relatively preserved; contrast sensitivity mildly reduced; stereo not testable due to manifest diplopia
- Ocular health: mild nuclear sclerosis OU; mild dermatochalasis OU; optic nerves healthy; no diabetic retinopathy; IOP normal
- Medical/medication context: Type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia; medications include metformin, antihypertensives, statin
- Driving: ceased driving due to diplopia
- Management plan (eye-care): GP systemic work-up; consideration of temporary occlusion or Fresnel prism for safer mobility; advised not to drive while diplopia present; single-vision distance correction advised for walking; review in 12 weeks or sooner if new neuro signs

2. Key Vision Issues

- Binocular diplopia at distance with left gaze, from left sixth nerve palsy, leading to impaired depth perception and stereopsis
  - Functional relevance: difficulty accurately judging distances and step/curb height while walking outdoors; risk of misjudging gaps or edges on footpaths
- Horizontal diplopia and abduction deficit
  - Functional relevance: limited binocular coordination during scanning across environments (e.g., intersections, uneven surfaces); may slow or disrupt safe navigation
- Mildly reduced contrast sensitivity
  - Functional relevance: poorer detection of low-contrast features (curb edges, shadowed surfaces, uneven pavement) especially in glare or low lighting
- Distance vision compromised with habitual bifocals; plan to use single-vision distance correction for walking
  - Functional relevance: walking tasks rely on distance clarity; switching eyewear use could affect consistency and stability during outdoor ambulation
- Visual field full; color vision intact; no RAPD; stable ocular health otherwise
  - Functional relevance: other visual information remains available; issues are primarily binocular coordination and depth cues
- Falls history: two confirmed outdoor falls on uneven footpaths; kerb negotiation difficulty reported
  - Functional relevance: history supports high concern for safety during outdoor mobility

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- Outdoor ambulation is likely at risk due to diplopia and reduced depth perception, particularly on uneven footpaths or kerbs; proximity judgments (e.g., curb height, step edges) may be unreliable
- Stopping driving reduces community mobility options and increases reliance on a cohabitant or public transport; may affect participation in errands, appointments, and social activities
- Compensatory behaviors (closing one eye) reduce stereoscopic depth cues and may further degrade depth judgment during complex walking tasks

ADLs/IADLs
- Outdoor shopping, banking, or appointments may become challenging without safe outdoor mobility; within-home tasks may be performed with assistance, but edges/thresholds still require safe negotiation when entering/exiting

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Reduced independent outdoor mobility may affect participation in community activities and exercise, potentially impacting social participation and conditioning
- Dependence on spouse for outdoor activities may influence routines and perceived independence

Environmental Interaction
- Outdoor environments (glare from headlights, variable lighting, wet or uneven surfaces) may exacerbate depth perception limitations and contrast sensitivity issues
- Kerbs and street edges require reliable distance judgment and edge detection

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

A. Self-care / Mobility
- Assess outdoor mobility safety in realistic route contexts (e.g., walking on familiar vs unfamiliar routes, crossing at mid-block vs intersections, kerb negotiation) to identify high-risk situations
- Consider whether an Orientation & Mobility (O&M) referral is appropriate to develop tailored strategies for community mobility with diplopia and reduced depth perception
- Assess whether environmental supports (e.g., consistent routes, clear curb markings, lighting considerations) would meaningfully reduce risk

B. Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- If home-to-community travel occurs, assess home entry/exit thresholds and approach paths for fall risks; recommendations may include lighting improvements or clutter reduction near entryways to reduce risk when transitioning to outdoor spaces
- Evaluate consistency of item placement and path lighting along primary routes to the car or bus stop

C. Lighting and Contrast
- Discuss how lighting conditions affect function (day vs night, glare from vehicle headlights); advise on strategies to minimize glare or shadows when moving outdoors (e.g., appropriate sunglasses, choosing well-lit routes)

D. Mobility Strategies
- O&M referral or consult to establish functional mobility goals, including safe route selection, environmental scanning strategies, and topography negotiation, without prescribing a fixed routine
- Emphasize pacing, reliance on predictable routes, and avoidance of high-risk environments during symptom peaks or low-visibility conditions

E. Task Adaptations
- Identify specific outdoor tasks that could be modified (e.g., time of day for walks; avoid uneven surfaces during peak diplopia; plan routes with smoother pavements)
- Consider whether educational emphasis on curb negotiation, stepping down from curbs, and recognizing edge contrast is needed

F. Assistive Technology / Non-visual Strategies
- Non-visual strategies may include consistent route planning, use of tactile or auditory cues in the environment, and reliance on partners or mobility aids when needed
- Eye-care management (e.g., temporary occlusion, prism decisions) should be coordinated with optometry; OT can support task adaptation around eyewear changes

G. Education and Support
- Educate the patient and spouse about safety strategies for outdoor activity, including when to seek assistance and how to recognize increasing diplopia or fatigue that increases fall risk
- Discuss contingency plans for poor visibility or unstable surfaces (e.g., choosing safer routes, avoiding walking alone at high-risk times)

H. Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Ongoing communication with the eye-care professional to monitor alignment and symptom changes; clarify when to escalate (e.g., persistent diplopia beyond expected recovery window, new neurological symptoms)
- Consider documentation of functional concerns to support multidisciplinary planning and referrals (e.g., to primary care for vascular risk assessment given microvascular etiology and diabetes)

I. Driving / Transport
- Since driving has ceased, emphasize alternative transportation planning to maintain community access; OT can assist with planning for shopping/appointments with the spouse or through community services

J. Falls Risk Management
- Given recurrent falls and diplopia, prioritize safety-focused assessment of outdoor mobility and consider fall-risk reduction strategies during OT evaluation planning

K. Multidisciplinary Liaison
- Coordinate with the optometrist for prescription considerations around walking glasses and any temporary occlusion/prism use; consider O&M input to optimize real-world mobility in parallel with eye-care management

5. Falls Risk Level

- Falls risk: High
- Rationale: recurrent outdoor falls on uneven footpaths, binocular diplopia with impaired depth perception during distance viewing, plus kerb-edge and curb-height judgment challenges; diplopia is a modifiable risk through vision management and OT environmental/compensation strategies, but remains a significant safety concern given outdoor mobility demands

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- The left sixth nerve palsy with distance diplopia and reduced depth perception is likely contributing to unsafe outdoor mobility, especially on uneven surfaces and curb negotiation; this increases falls risk and may limit community participation
- Compensatory behaviors (closing one eye) reduce binocular depth cues and could exacerbate difficulty with edge detection and obstacle avoidance during outdoor ambulation
- Vision is moderately compromised for walking due to distance vision and contrast sensitivity; planning for walking tasks should emphasize appropriate eyewear use and environmental strategies
- O&M referral consideration is appropriate to address safe public-mobility strategies without prescribing specific mobility techniques
- Driving cessation is already in place; OT planning should include transportation alternatives and community access supports to maintain independence
- Environmental considerations should focus on route lighting, glare management, and surface contrast where feasible in daily living and community activities

Any recommendations for specific mobility training or environmental modifications should be confirmed with the appropriate specialists and aligned with the patient’s goals and real-world context.

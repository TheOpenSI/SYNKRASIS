==============================
Case 066
==============================


1. **Patient Context**  
   * 71‑year‑old female, community living, active daily walker.  
   * Fluctuating visual clarity noted in the late afternoon/early evening; reports difficulty recognizing steps and kerbs when eyes feel irritated.  
   * No documented falls, but has self‑reported uncertainty about outdoor steps.  
   * Uses single‑vision distance spectacles for walking and readers for near work; no diplopia or field loss beyond stable glaucoma.  
   * Primary diagnosis: medication‑related ocular surface disease; secondary: glaucoma with stable intra‑ocular pressure.  
   * Prescribed multiple topical glaucoma drops; ocular surface findings (hyperemia, tear film instability, mild meibomian dysfunction) are driving late‑day visual fluctuation.

2. **Key Vision Issues**  
   * **Variable late‑day visual acuity and contrast sensitivity** – reduced CS (1.35 log units) and intermittent blur worsens with time of day.  
   * **Tear‑film instability** – contributes to fluctuating clarity, especially outdoors.  
   * **Uncertainty at kerbs/steps** – reported lack of confidence negotiating outdoor steps late in the day.  
   * **Limited use of vision‑enhancing aids for mobility** – relies on single‑vision distance glasses; no adaptive lighting or contrast measures reported.  
   * **Driving restriction to daytime only** – avoidance of late‑day driving due to irritation and blur.

3. **Functional Impact**  

   **Mobility and Falls**  
   * Variable acuity and contrast may impair safe navigation of uneven ground, steps, and kerbs, especially in dimmer evening lighting.  
   * Late‑day visual fluctuation may increase the risk of tripping or misjudging step depth, even without a prior fall.  
   * Self‑reported uncertainty suggests possible reduced confidence and hesitancy, which can alter gait patterns and increase fall risk.

   **ADLs/IADLs**  
   * **Outdoor walking** – may require additional visual cues or aids; may limit route selection or timing.  
   * **Driving** – restricted to daylight hours; may affect planning of errands, social visits, or access to services.  
   * **Reading signs or street signals** – may be impacted in low‑light or with glare, though not currently documented.  

   **Participation / Confidence / Roles**  
   * Potential for reduced participation in evening social activities or errands due to mobility uncertainty.  
   * Confidence in independent mobility may be affected; could lead to increased reliance on others or avoidance of certain environments.

   **Environmental Interaction**  
   * Outdoor lighting (street/sidewalk) may be insufficient during evening; glare from vehicles or headlights could exacerbate visual fluctuations.  
   * Indoor lighting during evening may be too low or uneven, affecting recognition of steps or obstacles.

4. **Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**

   **Mobility Strategies**  
   * *Assess* current walking gait and step negotiation in both daylight and evening conditions.  
   * *Consider* a structured mobility assessment, including obstacle negotiation and stair use, to identify compensatory strategies.  
   * *Educate* the client on safe walking practices: pause and scan before stepping, use of the cane if needed, and consistent lighting.  

   **Environmental Modifications**  
   * *Assess* outdoor environment for lighting adequacy (streetlights, path illumination) and step/kerb visibility.  
   * *Recommend* high‑contrast curb markers or tactile paving if feasible, and suggest use of brighter, adjustable lamps or reflective path strips in evening.  
   * *Advise* on maintaining clear pathways indoors and outdoors (clear walkways, removal of loose rugs).

   **Task Adaptation**  
   * *Explore* use of task lighting when walking in low‑light conditions (e.g., portable LED light).  
   * *Assess* whether the current single‑vision distance glasses provide adequate illumination for evening walking; consider if progressive or bifocal adaptations might reduce the need to switch spectacles.  
   * *Review* the timing of lubricant use – encourage use before outdoor activities to improve tear film stability.

   **Assistive Technology / Non‑visual Compensation**  
   * *Discuss* potential benefits of a smartphone audio‑navigation app for outdoor walking, especially in the evening.  
   * *Consider* auditory cues (e.g., foot‑step counting or pacing) as a non‑visual strategy for step negotiation.

   **Support & Education**  
   * *Provide* education on recognizing early signs of ocular irritation and the importance of timely lubricant application.  
   * *Inform* the client about the impact of late‑day visual fluctuation on safety, and encourage planning of outdoor activities during periods of better vision.  
   * *Encourage* the client to maintain a routine of eye‑care compliance to help stabilize visual clarity.

   **Driving / Transportation**  
   * *Document* the self‑restricted driving pattern and the underlying visual reason.  
   * *Assist* in planning alternative transport options for late‑day errands (public transport, rideshare, family assistance).  
   * *Support* exploration of a transportation safety plan that aligns with the client's visual limitations.

   **Inter‑professional Communication**  
   * *Coordinate* with the ophthalmologist/optometrist regarding the need for preservative‑free drops or lubricant strategy, as this directly influences OT‑identified mobility risks.  
   * *Highlight* that changes in medication timing or formulation may alter the timing of visual fluctuation, potentially affecting OT‑recommended safe activity schedules.

5. **Falls Risk Level** – *Moderate*  
   *Variable late‑day blur and reduced contrast increase the risk of tripping on uneven surfaces or steps, but no falls have occurred and field loss is stable.  

6. **Summary of Key OT Considerations**  
   * Assess and address mobility safety in late‑day conditions, focusing on step/kerb negotiation and environmental lighting.  
   * Provide education and strategies for lubricant use, task lighting, and adaptive footwear or aids to improve evening walking confidence.  
   * Coordinate with eye‑care professionals to align medication timing and visual stability with OT‑recommended activity planning.  
   * Support safe transportation planning for late‑day activities through alternative transport options and scheduling strategies.

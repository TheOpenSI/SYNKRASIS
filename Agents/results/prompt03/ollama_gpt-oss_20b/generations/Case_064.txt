==============================
Case 064
==============================


1. **Patient Context**

- 82‑year‑old male in a rehabilitation outpatient clinic after a right occipital stroke.  
- Recent single fall at home when turning into a left‑handed doorway; referral for OT assessment following this event.  
- Vision: right homonymous hemianopia with preserved central acuity (best corrected 6/7.5 OD/OS).  
- Uses bifocal spectacles; reports fatigue in busy environments, difficulty locating the start of text lines, and slower navigation in crowded spaces.  
- No diplopia, orthophoric at distance, 4 XP near.  
- Medical: atrial fibrillation, hypertension, anticoagulation; not driving.

2. **Key Vision Issues**

- **Left homonymous hemianopia** → loss of peripheral vision on the left side of both eyes, causing missed visual cues (e.g., doorway edges, people in the left field).  
- **Reduced contrast sensitivity** (1.35 log units at 1 m) → difficulty distinguishing low‑contrast edges and text, especially in poorly lit or cluttered areas.  
- **Bifocal spectacle use** → possible inconsistent use or reduced head‑turning speed to scan for left‑field objects; bifocal addition can create visual discontinuity during navigation.  
- **Mild meibomian gland dysfunction** → potential for transient blurring or dryness that may exacerbate visual fatigue in crowded settings.

3. **Functional Impact**

| Domain | Likely Functional Consequence (documented or inferred) |
|--------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| **Mobility & Falls** | Missed left‑side doorway edges → fall during turning; slower navigation in crowded indoor spaces; potential risk when turning around obstacles. |
| **ADLs/IADLs** | Difficulty locating the beginning of text lines → slower reading; possible challenges in reading medication labels or instructions when scanning left‑handed documents. |
| **Participation / Confidence** | Fatigue and missed cues in busy environments may limit confidence in community mobility, social outings, or shopping; potential avoidance of crowded spaces. |
| **Environmental Interaction** | Reduced contrast and field loss may make low‑contrast furniture edges, dim lighting, or cluttering particularly hazardous; bifocals may affect perception of floor transitions. |

4. **Vision‑Informed OT Considerations**

**Self‑care / Reading Tasks**  
- *Assess* whether left‑field visual scanning is sufficient to locate the start of lines during reading and medication label identification.  
- *Consider* use of high‑contrast, large‑print text or auditory reading aids if scanning is inadequate.  

**Household Management / Navigation**  
- *Assess* current home layout for left‑side clearance: doorway widths, corridor widths, and placement of furniture that might obstruct left‑hand side view.  
- *Environmental Modification*: recommend consistent furniture placement, high‑contrast door frames (e.g., contrasting paint or tape), and removal of left‑side clutter.  
- *Lighting & Contrast*: assess task lighting for reading areas and overall ambient light to improve edge visibility; consider adding task lights with adjustable brightness to reduce glare and improve contrast.  

**Mobility Strategies**  
- *Assess* effectiveness of current compensatory scanning and the use of a support guide.  
- *O&M Referral*: recommend an Orientation & Mobility assessment to refine left‑field scanning techniques, doorway negotiation, and safe corridor navigation.  
- *Assistive Device*: consider a white cane or pole with a white tip to enhance left‑side visual feedback during turning; discuss with the optometrist to confirm appropriateness.  

**Support Person Education**  
- *Educate* family/carer on maintaining a predictable, clear left‑hand side pathway: keeping walkways free of obstacles, placing frequent objects on the right side of the room where possible, and reinforcing agreed scanning habits.  
- *Inform* about potential impact of bifocals during movement; encourage consistent use or provide brief training on head‑turning strategies when transitioning between focal zones.  

**Participation & Community Access**  
- *Explore* whether the patient wishes to resume shopping or leisure activities in busy venues; assess risks and potential supports (e.g., escort, scheduled times during less busy periods).  
- *Driving*: reinforce that driving remains inappropriate until formal clearance; focus on alternative transport options if needed.  

**Non‑visual Compensation & Assistive Technology**  
- *Assistive Tech*: consider a simple visual cue system (e.g., contrasting stripes on steps or floor transitions) to aid in stair negotiation; discuss low‑vision identification badges for quick reference.  
- *Non‑visual*: train in auditory cues for navigation (e.g., using verbal prompts or audio‑feedback devices for doorways).  

**Medical & Ocular Health Context**  
- Monitor for fluctuations in visual comfort due to meibomian gland dysfunction; advise on ocular hygiene and timely review if vision fatigue increases.  

5. **Falls Risk Level**

- **Moderate–High**  
  *Rationale:* The patient has documented left hemianopia causing missed left‑side environmental cues, a recent fall directly linked to this deficit, and reduced contrast sensitivity. While central acuity is preserved, the combination of field loss, environmental challenges, and the presence of a fall suggests a significant vision‑related falls risk.  

6. **Summary of Key OT Considerations**

- Prioritise assessment of left‑field scanning effectiveness during door‑way and corridor navigation; refine strategies through O&M input.  
- Implement environmental modifications to enhance contrast and left‑side visibility, including high‑contrast door frames and clear pathways.  
- Evaluate and, if needed, introduce a white cane or support guide to improve left‑side edge detection during turning.  
- Provide support‑person education focused on maintaining predictable left‑side pathways and reinforcing scanning behaviours.  
- Address reading and medication‑label tasks with high‑contrast, large‑print materials or auditory aids if scanning remains inadequate.  

These considerations should guide the OT’s assessment and intervention planning while aligning with the patient’s current rehabilitation context and vision findings.

==============================
Case 072
==============================

1. Patient Context
- 82-year-old female, living independently with occasional family support; community optometry setting after GP referral.
- Primary eye condition: advanced dry age-related macular degeneration with geographic atrophy OU (foveal involvement OS; parafoveal OD). Pseudophakia OU.
- Functional concerns: reading medication labels, identifying objects directly ahead, face recognition, and meal preparation. One recent stumble in the kitchen (dark saucepan on a patterned floor mat) following difficulty noticing hazards.
- Vision aids: uses bifocals and a hand magnifier for fine print. No driving (ceased).

- Vision function summary (documented):
  - Distance VA: OD 6/60, OS 6/30, OU 6/30 at 6 m with bifocals; best-c corrected: OD 6/36, OS 6/24, OU 6/24.
  - Near VA: habitual N12 OU; with high add best corrected N6 OU at 20 cm.
  - Contrast sensitivity not testable due to reduced central acuity.
  - Visual field: peripheral fields full OU; central fixation tasks impaired OU.
  - Binocular alignment: orthophoria at distance; 4 prism diopters exophoria near.
  - Stereo acuity not testable; motility full OU; pupils normal; Ishihara normal OU (13/14).
  - Ocular health: mild blepharitis OU; intraocular pressure normal; macular GA with RPE atrophy OU; no exudation.
- Management plan (optometry-reported): routine retinal monitoring; low-vision referral for magnification, task lighting, and contrast; advised brighter kitchen lighting, improved object contrast, and caution with patterned/low-contrast items; review in 4 months or sooner if distortion or change.

2. Key Vision Issues
- Central vision loss from geographic atrophy OU with foveal involvement → major impact on reading, face recognition, and direct-ahead object identification; functionally contributes to difficulties with medication label reading, meal preparation, and noticing hazards directly in front.
- Reduced distance and near acuity even with correction; near vision available only with high-add magnification (habitual N12; best-corrected N6 at 20 cm with high add) → near tasks (reading labels, small print) require magnification and careful setup.
- Central fixation impairment OU with preserved peripheral fields → difficulty encoding items directly in front of the face; may affect hazard detection on a direct path; stereo acuity not testable.
- Patterned, low-contrast surfaces contributed to a recent near-miss (dark saucepan on patterned floor mat) → environment-related safety risk, especially for tasks performed in the kitchen.
- Colour vision largely preserved; overall ocular health stable aside from GA; no diplopia reported.

3. Functional Impact
Mobility and Falls
- In-home navigation, particularly on direct-ahead pathways with low-contrast or patterned flooring, may be impaired; direct-ahead hazard detection is reduced by central scotoma.
- History of a single kitchen fall suggests potential risk during meals preparation or object-search tasks in home environments; no documented outdoor mobility issues yet.

ADLs/IADLs
- Medication management: reading labels is challenging; may require magnification, large-print or high-contrast labeling, or non-visual reminders.
- Meal preparation: central vision loss and near-task magnification needs may limit ability to identify ingredients, read recipes, and safely carry out prep tasks.
- Face recognition: potential impact on safety and social participation, particularly with unfamiliar people or in new settings.
- Reading and following instructions or forms is compromised at near tasks; requires suitable lighting and contrast to support comprehension.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Independence in daily routines likely retained with supports, but confidence may be compromised in tasks requiring precise visual discrimination (e.g., reading small print, identifying items on patterned surfaces).
- Driving is not relevant; however, ongoing community participation depends on safe home-task performance and access to appropriate non-visual supports.

Environmental Interaction
- Bright, even lighting with high-contrast objects is important; patterned floor mats and low-contrast items directly ahead should be minimized or made more discernible.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations
A. ASSESS BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- Assess functional performance on key tasks in the home: reading medication labels, identifying foods/ingredients during meal prep, and locating objects on a typical prep surface.
- Observe mobility within a familiar home path and in areas with patterned or low-contrast flooring to gauge real-world hazard detection.
- Inventory current magnification and contrast strategies (hand magnifier use, lighting conditions) and determine if additional non-visual supports or high-contrast materials are feasible.

B. ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT AND MODIFICATION
- Lighting: evaluate task lighting at cooking/preparation surfaces; ensure even illumination with minimal glare; consider under-cabinet or adjustable lamp placement.
- Contrast and clutter: reduce reliance on patterned surfaces in key walking paths; improve contrast between objects and backgrounds (e.g., high-contrast border on floor mats, non-patterned floor in primary routes).
- Object placement: advocate consistent, predictable placement of frequently used items to simplify localization.
- Kitchen layout: assess for safe, clear pathways and potential tripping hazards; consider non-slip mats and removal of clutter around prep areas.

C. LIGHTING AND CONTRAST
- Recommend adjustable, glare-controlled task lighting with appropriate color temperature to optimize edge/contrast perception on objects (e.g., contrasting rims on containers, non-glossy surfaces).

D. MOBILITY STRATEGIES
- Given central vision impairment and a prior fall, consider referral to Orientation and Mobility (O&M) for a real-world mobility assessment if safety concerns extend to community or unfamiliar environments. Do not prescribe specific mobility routines; rely on specialist assessment for individualized strategies.

E. VISUAL + NON-VISUAL COMPENSATION / ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY
- Non-visual/low-vision supports: large-print or high-contrast medication labeling; consider talking or audio-supported reminders; smartphone accessibility features with large text and simple interfaces.
- Magnification: assess feasibility of additional magnification options (electronic pocket magnifier, camera-based systems) compatible with near tasks; ensure training and task-specific use.
- Task adaptation devices: color-contrasted pill organizers, clearly labeled containers, and consistent placement of frequently used items.

F. TASK ADAPTATION
- Medication management: evaluate how the patient identifies medications; adapt with high-contrast, large-print labels and an organized, consistent medication routine (e.g., simple pill box with color cues or audible reminders).
- Meal preparation: adjust workflow to minimize deep or difficult-to-see steps; use clearly labeled containers; plan tasks that can be completed with magnification and lighting support; consider staged cooking to reduce visual demand.

G. SUPPORT, CARERS AND FORMAL SERVICES
- Provide education to family/support persons about maintaining predictable kitchen layouts, ensuring adequate lighting, and reinforcing agreed strategies.
- Coordinate with low-vision services for expanded magnification/contrast training as appropriate; maintain communication with the ophthalmology team for monitoring and referrals.

H. PARTICIPATION AND COMMUNITY ACCESS
- Promote safe at-home participation in essential activities; plan for uninterrupted independence with appropriate environmental and task adaptations; re-evaluate if participation relies on altered routines or supports.

I. DRIVING / LICENSING / TRANSPORT
- Not applicable based on current information; continue to respect driving cessation and support alternatives.

J. EYEWEAR FUNCTION
- Reinforce consistent use of current bifocals; assess whether bifocal use affects near-task performance or alignment during tasks, and discuss with the optometrist if any optical strategy adjustment is warranted (without prescribing optical changes).

K. LOW VISION SERVICE
- Acknowledge and coordinate with the low-vision referral for magnification and contrast training; ensure interim OT strategies address immediate functional needs.

L. BEHAVIOURAL ADAPTATION
- If use of magnification or lighting is inconsistent, implement habit-based strategies: fixed spots for magnifiers, routine checks for lighting before task initiation, and predictable placement of high-contrast tools.

M. FLUCTUATING VISION
- Not indicated in this case.

N. FEASIBILITY
- Environmental and non-visual strategies (lighting adjustments, labeling changes, consistent object placement) are feasible in this setting; extensive home modifications are unlikely needed at this time.

O. PRIORITISATION
- Safety-critical risks: misreading labels and hazards in the direct-ahead path due to central vision loss.
- High-frequency tasks: medication management, meal preparation, and basic self-care activities.
- Modifiable factors: lighting, contrast, and environmental layout; non-visual compensations; task adaptations.
- Potential referrals: O&M for mobility safety as needed; low-vision services for magnification/contrast optimization.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate: one fall with central vision loss and low-contrast hazard in the home; not solely vision, but visually mediated risk is present and warrants targeted assessment and environmental strategies.

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings
- Central vision loss from GA OU with foveal involvement severely impacts reading, face recognition, and direct-ahead object identification; magnification and high-contrast/lighting strategies are essential for functional tasks (medication labels, meal prep).
- In-home safety at direct-ahead hazards on patterned/low-contrast surfaces requires environmental optimization (lighting, contrast, and floor-pattern modification) to reduce misperception and falls risk.
- Primary OT focus areas: medication management and meal preparation (near tasks), with attention to consistent, high-contrast labeling and task lighting; consider non-visual strategies and supports while coordinating with low-vision services.
- Consider O&M referral if broader mobility safety becomes a concern, and maintain liaison with eye-care for ongoing monitoring and magnification/contrast optimization.

Note: All recommendations are contingent on documented vision findings and standard OT reasoning; ophthalmologic management remains the domain of eye care.

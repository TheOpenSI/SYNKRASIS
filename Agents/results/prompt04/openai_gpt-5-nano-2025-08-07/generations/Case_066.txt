==============================
Case 066
==============================


1. Patient Context

- Age 71, female; community optometry practice setting.
- History-driven symptoms: six months of fluctuating blur, stinging, and watering, worse later in the day; reports uncertainty negotiating kerbs and outdoor steps in late afternoon; no confirmed fall.
- Vision aids: uses single-vision distance spectacles for walking and readers for near tasks.
- Eye-health context: long-standing glaucoma on multi-drop therapy; ocular surface disease likely related to topical therapy (preservative hypersensitivity suspected) with tear-film instability and meibomian gland dysfunction; early nuclear sclerosis OU.
- Visual function snapshot: distance acuity with habitual correction declines late in day; best-corrected distance acuity modestly better; near vision with readers but still affected by tear-film symptoms; reduced contrast sensitivity; stereoacuity limited; no diplopia; full fields on confrontation.
- Driving: daytime driver; avoids late-day driving when irritation is present.
- Management plan ( ophthalmology-related): consider preservative-free formulations; lubricant use advised; communication with ophthalmologist to determine medication changes or review timing; follow-up in 6 weeks to reassess ocular surface, compliance, IOP, and functional impact.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Fluctuating/late-day visual clarity due to ocular surface disease from glaucoma-topical therapy, with tear-film instability and surface staining.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity and limited depth perception (stereo 80" at 40 cm) potentially affecting edge/step recognition, particularly in outdoor, late-day lighting.
- Distance and near vision show residual impairment even with correction, superimposed by symptoms (stinging, watering) that interfere with sustained clarity.
- Binocular status is near-oriented exotropia without diplopia; overall functional vision is impacted more by surface symptoms than by gross field loss.
- Environmental and task context: outdoor mobility relies on kerb/step negotiation; daytime driving retained but late-day driving risk increases with symptoms.

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- Late-day variability in vision may compromise safe navigation of curbs, steps, and uneven surfaces, especially in outdoor or unfamiliar environments; potential increase in hesitation or skillful scanning requirements during duskier lighting or where glare/shadowing is present.
ADLs/IADLs
- Reading labels, medication instructions, and small print near tasks may be intermittently challenging during symptomatic periods; may affect ability to read digital/printed materials without strategy modification.
Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Activity participation may become more cautious or time-consuming in late day; daytime routine remains possible, but confidence for evening activities could be reduced.
Environmental Interaction
- Lighting, glare, and contrast are pivotal given tear-film instability and edge contrast on steps; inconsistent environmental lighting could amplify functional difficulty.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

Self-care
- Medication management: assess ability to identify and read glaucoma eye-drop bottles and instruction labels across the day, particularly during late-day symptoms; consider non-visual or high-contrast labeling strategies and routine cues to reduce medication-administration errors.

Mobility Strategies
- Consider an O&M referral to evaluate safe community mobility with fluctuating vision, focusing on kerb and step negotiation, route planning, and strategies for low-contrast environments; avoid prescribing fixed mobility routines without individual assessment.
- Evaluate how late-day variability interacts with outdoor tasks (e.g., crossing streets, negotiating unfamiliar routes) to determine need for supervision, assistance, or travel planning changes.

Environmental Modifications / Lighting
- Recommend comprehensive environmental assessment focusing on lighting quality and glare control, contrast on steps/curbs, and consistent placement of commonly used objects (e.g., handrails, walkway lighting) to improve edge visibility and reduce missteps.
- Discuss use of task lighting and avoidance of high-glare environments during symptomatic periods; consider high-contrast edge cues for stairs/thresholds.

Visual + Non-visual Compensation / Assistive Technology
- If reading tasks become unreliable during late-day fluctuations, explore non-visual or low-vision strategies (e.g., large-print materials, clear contrast in labels) and usable assistive technologies aligned with goals and feasibility.
- Encourage consistent use of lubricants or artificial tears prior to outdoor activities as advised by ophthalmology, to potentially stabilize function during walking.

Task Adaptation
- Identify at-risk tasks (e.g., reading medication labels at the pharmacy, identifying objects outdoors on steps) and plan for assessment of specific steps taken during symptomatic periods; avoid assuming device choices—focus on task-based adaptations.

Education and Support
- Prepare patient and caregiver education about vision fluctuation risks, strategies to maintain safety during late-day activities, and when to seek ophthalmology input for medication-related ocular surface symptoms.
- Discuss support planning for times and environments with higher vision risk (e.g., darker or busier outdoor settings).

Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Maintain communication with optometry/ophthalmology regarding medication changes and their potential functional impact; coordinate with the patient for timely reassessment of functional mobility and safety in 6 weeks.

Driving / Transport
- Reinforce the documented plan to limit late-day driving during symptomatic periods; discuss alternative arrangements for evening activities or appointments if needed.

Be mindful of non-visual factors (fatigue, chronic illness burden, polypharmacy) that may interact with vision symptoms and affect safety and participation.

5. Falls Risk Level

- Moderate falls risk (vision-related), given fluctuating late-day vision with reduced contrast sensitivity and edge-edge visibility on steps, combined with kerb/step negotiation uncertainty in outdoor environments. No documented falls yet; risk is linked to symptom fluctuations and environmental factors.

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- Mobility safety with late-day vision fluctuations: consider O&M assessment to evaluate curb/step negotiation and overall community mobility in low-contrast or variable lighting; address potential impact on safety and participation.
- Medication management and near tasks: assess ability to read and interpret glaucoma medication labeling and instructions across the day; ensure practical strategies for reliable medication administration during symptomatic periods.
- Environmental and lighting supports: consider comprehensive lighting/contrast strategies for home and outdoor routes (glare control, step-edge visibility, consistent layout) to enhance safety and confidence during late-day tasks.
- Education and collaboration: engage patient (and caregiver, if appropriate) in education about managing ocular-surface symptoms to minimize functional impact and coordinate with eye care for potential medication adjustments and plans.

End of OT summary for Case 066.

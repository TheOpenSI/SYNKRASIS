==============================
Case 032
==============================


1. Patient Context

- Age 80, male, community-dwelling.
- Recent onset left sixth nerve palsy with binocular horizontal diplopia, worse at distance and in left gaze.
- Depth perception impairment (functional stereopsis loss) identified as a key contributor to outdoor falls; two falls on uneven footpaths in the past 4 months; difficulty judging kerb height; hesitancy stepping off curbs.
- Uses bifocals full-time; no headaches, ptosis, or acute neurological symptoms.
- Driving ceased after diplopia became frequent; reduces outdoor walking alone; relies on wife for mobility.
- Medical context: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia; medications include metformin, antihypertensives, statin.
- Vision findings include: distance diplopia with left gaze, left abduction deficit, mild contrast sensitivity reduction, full visual fields, and normal color vision; best-c corrected distance acuity is reduced to approximately 6/9 OU; stereo is not testable due to manifest deviation.
- Optometry-management plan includes consideration of temporary occlusion or Fresnel prism for safer mobility and a review in ~12 weeks; not driving during diplopia; single-vision distance correction recommended for walking tasks.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Binocular diplopia and impaired depth perception at distance due to left sixth nerve palsy with left gaze involvement, compromising accurate distance/obstacle judgments during outdoor ambulation.
- Left abduction deficit with horizontal diplopia in distance gaze, limiting reliable alignment and depth cues; stereopsis cannot be relied upon for depth discrimination.
- Mildly reduced contrast sensitivity, which can hinder edge/curb detection and obstacle discrimination in outdoor lighting or cluttered environments.
- Full visual fields but inability to test stereo means depth cues are uncertain; reliance on non-stereo cues for distance/edge detection may be necessary.
- Full-time bifocal wear during walking may contribute to misalignment or transient diplopia on gaze shifts; optometry plan supports switching to single-vision distance correction for walking tasks.

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- Outdoor ambulation is affected by impaired distance perception and depth cues, particularly when negotiating kerbs, uneven surfaces, and parked-car areas; two recent falls on uneven footpaths illustrate real-world risk.
- Difficulty judging kerb height and step-off edges may increase hesitancy and utilization of support (partner assistance, slower pace, or avoidance of single-handed outdoor navigation).

ADLs/IADLs
- No specific ADL/IADL tasks described as impaired; however, ongoing outdoor mobility safety and device use for navigation are relevant to daily routines outside the home.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Reduced community participation due to fear of falls and diplopia; cessation of driving further limits independent mobility and social participation; reliance on spouse for outings.

Environmental Interaction
- Outdoor tasks are sensitive to lighting and surface conditions; glare and surface contrast may further impact edge/curb visibility, though these factors are not explicitly documented in the case.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

Self-care
- Not a primary concern inside the home, but may be influenced by future mobility needs if outdoor access is limited or if environmental barriers remain.

Mobility Strategies
- Consider referral to Orientation and Mobility (O&M) assessment/training to tailor safe community ambulation with diplopia and depth-perception deficits. O&M may address scanning strategies and route considerations without prescribing specific mobility routines here.
- Advise coordination of mobility plans with the eye-care team, particularly regarding alignment status and recovery trajectory, to optimize safe outdoor movement.

Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- Assess the patient’s most-used outdoor routes for lighting adequacy, glare, shadowing, surface quality, and curb visibility; provide guidance to select safer routes and times of day with better lighting.
- Recommend environmental support during ambulation such as consistent, unobstructed paths and clearly marked curb edges in frequented routes (consider simple, practical adjustments rather than extensive home modifications).

Lighting and Contrast
- Consider recommendations to optimize lighting on outdoor routes (adequate even illumination, reduced glare) and high-contrast cues at curb edges or steps when feasible.

Mobility Strategies and Task Adaptations
- Promote use of distance-only glasses for walking to reduce diplopia during gait and curb negotiation; this aligns with optometry guidance and OT safety principles.
- Encourage deliberate pacing, increased head/eye scanning to anticipate curb edges; however, avoid prescriptive mobility routines and defer individualized strategies to O&M or specialist training.
- Consider non-visual strategies and environmental cues (tactile/tactile-ground indicators where applicable, predictable routes) to bolster safety when vision reliability is variable.

Assistive Technology / Non-visual Strategies
- Non-visual navigation aids (phone-based navigation cues, audible street-crossing prompts) may support independence, particularly when diplopia limits visual cues; ensure usability and training needs are considered.
- Do not prescribe specific devices without multidisciplinary coordination; emphasize OT-facilitated task setup and training rather than device selection.

Education and Support
- Education for patient and spouse about safe outdoor mobility, recognizing symptom fluctuation, and when to avoid independent ambulation (e.g., poor visibility, uneven surfaces, or when diplopia worsens).
- Discuss transportation options and contingency plans given driving cessation.

Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Coordinate follow-up with GP/optometry for the expected recovery trajectory and systemic risk factors (diabetes control, BP, lipids) that influence cranial nerve palsy recovery.
- 12-week review as planned by eye care; consider OT communication to review functional implications if diplopia persists or mobility safety concerns continue.

Driving / Transport
- Driving cessation is documented; OT considerations may include planning for alternative transportation, maintaining community access, and safety education for non-driving mobility.

Eye health context
- Monitor for potential progression or resolution of diplopia and depth perception with ongoing medical management; OT focuses on functional adaptation rather than medical treatment.

5. Falls Risk Level

Moderate falls risk: Documented outdoor falls related to distance diplopia/depth perception with kerb-height estimation challenges in the context of a recent binocular motor deficit and reduced outdoor independence; no home-based fall history is described, but outdoor ambulation risk is evident.

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- Prioritize outdoor mobility safety assessment and potential O&M referral to tailor strategies for negotiating kerbs, uneven surfaces, and distance judgments given left sixth nerve palsy with diplopia and depth-perception impairment.
- Implement vision-informed task adaptations for walking: switch to single-vision distance correction for walking tasks to reduce diplopia during ambulation, with OT support around consistent spectacle use and safe navigation habits.
- Plan environmental and lighting considerations to minimize glare and shadowing in outdoor routes; emphasize consistent routes with good contrast and clear edges to support edge detection.
- Address participation and independence: develop a plan for safe community mobility and transportation options in light of driving cessation, with education for the patient and spouse and consideration of support needs.
- Coordinate care with optometry and GP, including monitoring recovery trajectory, BP and metabolic control, and potential re-evaluation of glasses prescription as symptoms evolve.

Note: This summary translates the optometry findings into OT-relevant functional implications, flags areas to assess, and suggests non-prescriptive, collaboration-focused considerations. It does not replace optometric management or formal OT assessment.

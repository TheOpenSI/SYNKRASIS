Case 035

1. Patient Context

- 67-year-old man, shared-care glaucoma review in primary/optometry setting.
- Ophthalmology-managed glaucoma OU with progression from suspect to established disease over several years.
- Chief concern: difficulty noticing floor-level hazards until late; reports diminished confidence stepping around low furniture and in darker areas over the past approximately 9 months.
- Vision correction: uses single-vision distance spectacles for walking; readers for near tasks.
- Visual function: central vision reasonably preserved with current correction; peripheral/field loss consistent with glaucoma; reduced contrast sensitivity; stereo depth reduced; no diplopia or central blur; mild nuclear sclerosis; mild conjunctival hyperemia from treatment.
- Falls/referral context: routine review prompted by worsening obstacle awareness; several near-misses in dim hallways; no documented falls.
- Driving: drives daytime and occasionally at dusk.
- Medical history: hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea.
- Current management plan: glaucoma therapy consideration (additional IOP-lowering agent or MIGS with cataract approach) with ophthalmology follow-up; environmental safety recommendations provided; referral to Orientation & Mobility (O&M) / Occupational Therapy (OT) advised.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Peripheral field loss from glaucoma progression (inferior/ arcuate defects) with overall functional impact beyond central acuity.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity, particularly affecting detection of low-contrast floor-level hazards.
- Reduced stereopsis (depth perception) which may influence safe edge/step judgments in real-world settings.
- Central vision and reading vision remain usable with current correction; no central blur or diplopia reported.
- Environment-related risk factors (dim lighting, floor clutter) likely amplify the functional impact of the visual findings.
- Driving status includes daytime and some nighttime/dusk exposure; governing safety considerations rely on ophthalmology, but OT can plan around safety implications.

3. Functional Impact

Mobility and Falls
- In dim hallways, reduced obstacle awareness and floor-level hazard detection may increase near-miss risk and potential trips, especially around low furniture and in low-contrast floor areas.
- Peripheral field loss and diminished depth perception could affect safe navigation in familiar indoor routes and in transitional spaces (e.g., doorway thresholds, steps) under suboptimal lighting.

ADLs/IADLs
- No explicit ADL deficit documented; however, tasks requiring quick visual scanning in dim lighting (e.g., locating objects on dark floors, reading in low contrast environments) may be affected.
- Medication management and meals not explicitly described as impaired, but organization/label recognition in dim lighting could be indirectly influenced by reduced contrast perception.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Activity participation in dim environments (e.g., evening or night activities in the home or community) may be limited by safety concerns or reduced confidence navigating low-contrast hazards.
- Driving-related participation may be affected in low-light conditions; formal driving fitness remains a medical decision, but OT considerations include planning for safer alternatives if needed.

Environmental Interaction
- Home environment with dim lighting and floor clutter is a potential safety risk; higher-contrast, well-lit paths may mitigate risk.
- Floor-level hazards (low furniture, rugs, changes in flooring) particularly problematic under reduced contrast.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

Self-care
- Evaluate safe navigation within the home in common dim-light scenarios (e.g., entering/exiting rooms, moving around low furniture, stepping over thresholds).
- Consider how dim lighting and floor clutter might affect self-care routines performed at night.

Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- Assess and address home lighting quality across key routes (hallways, stairs/step edges, thresholds) including even illumination and glare control.
- Implement clutter reduction and ensure consistent furniture placement to create predictable pathways.
- Recommend strategies to enhance floor-edge visibility (e.g., high-contrast edge cues on stairs/step edges; clear, uncluttered floor space).
- Consider high-contrast, clearly labeled or organized storage for commonly used items to support orientation.

Lighting and Contrast
- Assess overall lighting levels, glare, and transitions between lighting zones; optimize lighting to reduce shadows and improve edge/step visibility without creating glare.
- Promote task lighting in key areas (e.g., hallways, approach to furniture, entry/exit points) to support safer visual scanning.

Mobility Strategies
- Referral to Orientation & Mobility for functional mobility assessment and individualized scanning/route planning in low-contrast environments; emphasis on safe navigation within the home and immediate surroundings rather than formal step-by-step routines.
- Explore development of a consistent home mobility plan that prioritizes well-lit, uncluttered routes with minimal abrupt lighting changes.

Task Adaptations
- Advise organizing frequently used items in high-contrast, predictable locations to reduce search time in dim conditions.
- Consider environmental strategies that reduce reliance on peripheral vision for hazard detection (e.g., clear pathways, unobstructed floor, strategic placement of lighting).

Assistive Technology / Non-visual Strategies
- Non-visual cues (auditory/tactile) and predictable environmental layouts can support safe navigation when vision is limited.
- Encourage the use of enhanced ambient lighting and potentially contrast-appropriate markers or floor indicators as tolerated and feasible, coordinated with low-vision/eye-care input as appropriate.

Education and Support
- Educate patient and family about safety strategies in dim lighting, including use of extra lighting, consistent routes through the home, and planning for higher-risk tasks during times of better visibility.
- Discuss the potential need for supervision or assistance during high-risk activities (e.g., navigating home at night, moving through cluttered spaces).

Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Coordinate with ophthalmology to monitor glaucoma progression and review treatment adjustments; communicate vision-related safety implications to inform OT planning.
- Consider a formal O&M assessment to tailor mobility strategies to the person’s home environment and routine.

Driving / Transport
- Given daytime driving and occasional dusk driving, document functional implications for driving safety with the eye-care team; discuss transport alternatives or safety planning if progression or symptoms worsen. OT notes are not a fitness-to-drive decision; it remains an ophthalmology-driven domain with OT support for community mobility planning.

Eye-wear Function
- No recommendation for eyewear changes is within OT scope; translate current eyewear use (central correction) into functional implications such as stable distance vision for outdoor mobility and staying within familiar routes; emphasize consistent spectacle use to maintain predictable vision during tasks.

Low Vision / Low-Vision Service
- Acknowledge the potential for low-vision input if functional tasks become more detail-dependent or if progression limits functional performance despite correction. Do not prescribe during OT scope; liaise with appropriate eye-care services as needed.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate: Peripheral field loss with demonstrated near-misses in dim lighting suggests a non-trivial risk of falls or near-falls, amplified by low-contrast floor hazards. Not expressed as a confirmed fall, but risk is functionally meaningful and warrants proactive safety planning.

5. Falls Risk Level
- Moderate
- Rationale: Peripheral field loss with near-misses in dim hallways indicates a meaningful mobility safety risk in low-contrast environments, though no formal fall history is documented.

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- Peripheral field loss and reduced contrast sensitivity in dim environments are likely contributing to obstacle and floor-level hazard awareness challenges, increasing safety risk during indoor mobility.
- Prioritize home lighting optimization, clutter reduction, and floor-edge visibility to support safer navigation and reduce near-misses.
- Recommend an OT- or O&M-led mobility assessment to tailor strategies for identifying hazards, scanning approaches, and route planning within the home and immediate surroundings; avoid prescriptive mobility routines without individualized assessment.
- Discuss driving safety implications with the ophthalmology team and plan for transportation alternatives or supports if deterioration progresses; OT can support community-access planning and safety education.
- Consider referral to low-vision services if progression leads to task-specific functional limitations (e.g., detail-dependent tasks), while continuing to focus on environmental adaptations and non-visual compensations in the interim.

Notes for clinical handover
- Vision findings translate into a clear safety focus on dim-light navigation and floor-level hazard detection.
- OT priority areas: environmental modifications (lighting and clutter control); mobility assessment and training (O&M input as appropriate); patient and caregiver education; and coordination with ophthalmology for ongoing disease management.
- No changes to optometric management are prescribed here; maintain joint planning with eye-care providers.

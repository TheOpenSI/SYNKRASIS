==============================
Case 072
==============================

1. Patient Context

- 82-year-old female living independently in the community; occasional family support.
- Primary ocular diagnosis: advanced dry age-related macular degeneration with geographic atrophy OU (foveal/parafoveal involvement) causing central vision loss.
- Vision-related functional status: marked reduction in distance and near vision, with central scotoma in both eyes; uses bifocals and a hand magnifier for fine print.
- Driving status: has ceased driving.
- Falls history: history of one fall in the kitchen after not clearly seeing a dark saucepan on a patterned floor mat directly ahead; no recurrent falls reported.
- Ocular health: bilateral pseudophakia; central macular atrophy with RPE atrophy OU; no exudation or hemorrhage; OCT confirms outer retinal and RPE atrophy.
- Current management context: referred by GP for retinal assessment; routine ophthalmology follow-up planned; a low-vision referral was initiated for magnification, lighting, and contrast strategies.
- Functional concerns reported by patient: progressively patchy central vision affecting reading, face recognition, and meal preparation; difficulty with medication label reading; falls risk related to hazards in home environment.
- Education/previous care gaps: has not attended retinal review for over a year.

2. Key Vision Issues

- Central vision loss from geographic atrophy OU with foveal involvement → marked reduction in distance and near acuity; central scotomas limit detailed recognition of objects and faces.
- Reduced functional ability to read medication labels and perform near tasks without magnification; near acuity currently requiring high adds for close work.
- Difficulty with direct-ahead object identification and hazard detection in real-world environments (e.g., kitchen), contributing to a reported fall in a patterned, low-contrast setting.
- Contrast sensitivity not testable in clinic due to central acuity limitations; general report of reduced detail discernment and trouble distinguishing objects against patterned backgrounds.
- Colour vision intact; no diplopia; peripheral fields largely preserved but central fixation impaired OU.

3. Functional Impact

Falls and Mobility
- Central vision loss with central scotomas may hinder obstacle detection and direct-ahead hazard recognition in familiar and unfamiliar environments, particularly with clutter, patterned floors, or low-contrast items.
- One kitchen-related fall suggests risk in-home during routine tasks that require detecting small or low-contrast hazards in the direct line of sight.

ADLs/IADLs
- Reading and interpreting medication labels is impaired, affecting safe medication management.
- Meal preparation and kitchen tasks (e.g., locating utensils, identifying ingredients, safely handling hot items) are at risk due to reduced central vision and contrast discrimination.
- Face recognition is affected, which may influence social interaction safety and daily routines that rely on identifying people.
- Near work and detail-dependent tasks require magnification and high-contrast supports; completion of some tasks may take longer or require adaptive strategies.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Independence at home is feasible with adaptations, but confidence may be reduced for tasks requiring precise visual detail, potentially affecting participation in certain activities (e.g., complex meal prep, reading receipts, recognizing familiar faces in dim lighting).
- No current driving; reliance on alternative transport or supports may be needed for broader participation if outside activities are pursued.

Environmental Interaction
- Brightness and contrast in the home (notably kitchen) impact task safety; patterned floor surfaces and low-contrast objects directly ahead pose additional risk.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate. Rationale: single fall in a high-risk, low-contrast, patterned environment with central vision loss, plus ongoing risk for reading and hazard detection in daily activities. Not a confirmed high-risk profile at this time, but modifiable with environmental and task adaptations.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations

Self-care
- Medication management: assess ability to read current medication labels and doses; consider non-visual strategies (high-contrast labeling, large-print instructions, organized medication storage in a consistent, accessible location). Explore feasibility of high-contrast pill organizers or talking labels as adjuncts if available and aligned with patient goals.
- Meal preparation and kitchen safety: assess current kitchen layout and task sequence for high-risk steps (e.g., turning on stove, handling hot items, recognizing cookware and timer cues). Recommend environmental supports to enhance safety and efficiency during cooking and food preparation.

Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- Lighting and contrast: assess kitchen and main activity areas for even, glare-free illumination; ensure task lighting is bright and evenly distributed and positioned to reduce shadows at the work surface. Avoid reliance on patterns that confuse perception of hazards.
- Contrast and clutter: minimize clutter and patterned surfaces directly ahead (e.g., avoid patterned floor mats in direct walking paths); provide high-contrast cues for edges of steps, doorway thresholds, and commonly used objects (e.g., utensil racks, appliance handles) to improve identification.
- Household object placement: advocate for consistent placement of frequently used items to reduce search time and confusion.

Mobility Strategies
- Given central vision loss with a documented direct-ahead hazard, consider referral to orientation & mobility (O&M) evaluation if outside-the-home mobility or new environments are anticipated; within the home, strategies may focus on scanning habits and environmental layout adjustments rather than scripted mobility routines.
- Emphasize safe in-home navigation strategies and fall-prevention practices in areas with potential hazards (kitchen, entryways).

Task Adaptations
- Develop task approaches that minimize near-visual demands for high-risk activities (e.g., medication management and cooking). Break tasks into simple steps, use consistent sequencing, and rely on non-visual cues where possible (timers, textured cues, labeled containers).

Assistive Technology / Non-Visual Strategies
- Non-visual options may include: magnification and high-contrast materials for near tasks; bright, large-print instructions; voice-guided or smartphone accessibility features as appropriate; talking devices for timers/reminders if aligned with goals and training.
- Any technology recommendations should be considered in collaboration with low-vision services and tailored to the patient’s abilities and preferences.

Education and Support
- Educate patient and informal supports about safety strategies, consistent environments, and predictable routines to reduce risk.
- Provide caregiver/family guidance on maintaining clear walkways, consistent storage, and reinforcing agreed strategies.

Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Coordinate with ophthalmology/low-vision services to optimize magnification, lighting and contrast strategies, and to schedule timely retina reviews.
- Consider a formal low-vision assessment pathway for optimization of reading and near tasks if not already in place.
- Monitor for new symptoms suggestive of progression (e.g., new distortion) and adjust OT considerations accordingly.

Information Gaps / Assessment Needs
- Assess home environment specifics (kitchen layout, flooring surfaces, clutter levels, and task-specific lighting) to tailor environmental modifications.
- Confirm current use of magnification or assistive devices in daily tasks and whether patient has access to high-contrast labeling or talking devices.
- Determine patient goals and priorities (e.g., whether she wants to re-engage in some community activities or improve specific tasks at home) to structure OT focus.

Notes on scope
- Do not attempt to replace optometry management; continue collaboration with ophthalmology/low-vision services.
- Do not prescribe ophthalmic devices; translate vision findings into OT-relevant functional implications and environmental/compensatory strategies.

5. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings

- Central vision loss with foveal geographic atrophy leads to difficulty reading medications, identifying faces, and performing near tasks; increases risk for misreading labels and unsafe meal preparation.
- In-home safety is impacted by low-contrast and patterned surfaces; a single fall occurred in a kitchen context, highlighting hazard exposure in direct-ahead tasks.
- Environmental and task adaptations (lighting, contrast, and consistent object placement) are likely to meaningfully improve safety and independence in ADLs and IADLs.
- Referral to or coordination with low-vision services and, if needed, O&M evaluation may support safer and more confident community participation.
- Education and caregiver involvement should focus on predictable environments and hazard reduction within the home.

6. Key OT Priorities (concise synthesis)

- Enhance home lighting and contrast in key areas (especially kitchen) and reduce reliance on patterned backgrounds to improve direct-ahead object visibility and hazard detection.
- Implement practical medication and meal-prep supports (high-contrast labeling, consistent storage, organized workflows) to reduce safety risk with core tasks.
- Explore non-visual strategies and appropriate low-vision tools (magnification supports, large-print labels, audible reminders) in collaboration with low-vision services.
- Consider a home-environment assessment and, if needed, an O&M referral for broader mobility safety in unfamiliar settings; ensure communications with eye-care providers for integrated care.
- Engage patient and family in education about safety, routine planning, and environmental consistency to sustain safe participation at home.

End of Case 072 OT summary.

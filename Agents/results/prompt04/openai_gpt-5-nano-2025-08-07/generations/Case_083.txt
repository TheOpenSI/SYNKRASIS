==============================
Case 083
==============================

1. Patient Context
- 78-year-old female in a shared-care glaucoma review pathway (community optometry).
- Diagnosis: normal-tension glaucoma OU with progression affecting the inferior visual field in both eyes.
- Functional description: increasing difficulty detecting objects approaching from the side, especially to the right; previously independent at home but more cautious in cluttered environments; reports clipping doorframes and laundry baskets.
- Vision correction: uses single-vision distance spectacles for walking and separate readers for near tasks.
- Driving: currently drives short daytime distances; advised to refrain from driving until a formal driving assessment (Esterman) is completed.
- Medical context: hypertension, migraine, Raynaud’s phenomenon; on antihypertensives.
- Ocular findings of note: progressive, structure–function concordant glaucomatous damage with reduced contrast sensitivity, shallow peripheral field function, and mild nucleus sclerosis that may affect visual quality; no RAPD; stereo depth reduced;-binocularity generally stable but near exophoria present.

2. Key Vision Issues
- Progressive inferior/peripheral visual field loss OU with side-collision risk: results in reduced awareness of objects/people approaching from the side, particularly in hallway and cluttered spaces.
- Reduced contrast sensitivity: may hinder detection of low-contrast obstacles or edge cues in dim or busy environments, contributing to collision risk.
- Impaired depth perception (stereo) and paracentral/offset field loss: can affect judging distance to objects during turning or when negotiating obstacles in motion.
- Driving restriction due to field loss: functional implication for community mobility and access to alternatives; requires planning for transport and safety education.
- Visual symptoms with refractive correction: no major central blur or diplopia reported; color vision borderline—less central to immediate OT actions but may influence reading/labeling or task screening in complex environments.

3. Functional Impact
Mobility and Falls
- Hallway navigation and turning in cluttered environments may require slower, more deliberate scanning to detect right-sidestuff; increased risk of side-doorframe/laundry-basket contacts during movement.
- Reduced contrast and depth cues may contribute to hesitancy, particularly when negotiating thresholds, steps, or low-contrast flooring transitions.

ADLs/IADLs
- Locating items in cluttered spaces and reading low-contrast labels or signs may be more challenging, especially in poorer lighting or busy environments.
- Reading-to-walk transitions and devices that rely on accurate depth or edge detection may be affected.

Participation / Confidence / Roles
- Driving restriction and the need for safer community mobility planning may influence independence and social participation; potential need for alternative transport arrangements.
- Ongoing cautiousness in cluttered or unfamiliar environments may affect engagement in some routine activities outside the home.

Environmental Interaction
- Hallway lighting quality, glare, and clutter directly interact with functional performance; variable lighting conditions may amplify functional difficulty.

4. Vision-Informed OT Considerations
Self-care
- Not a primary focus, but assess whether any self-care tasks (e.g., medication organization, bathroom navigation) are affected by side-field loss in dim or cluttered environments; reinforce safe routines if needed.

Household Management
- Consider whether clutter reduction and consistent item placement could reduce near-miss events; encourage predictable kitchen/drawer organization to support item localization.

Environmental Modifications / Home Environment
- Assess and support practical environmental adjustments to reduce risk:
  - Lighting: ensure even hallway illumination with minimal glare and shadowing; consider task lighting at entry/turn points; avoid abrupt lighting transitions.
  - Clutter: reinforce decluttering and clear travel paths; maintain consistent furniture placement to support predictable routes.
  - Contrast/Edges: use high-contrast markings on doorways, edges, and steps if applicable; consider simple environmental cues that enhance edge detection.

Lighting and Contrast
- Evaluate and optimize area lighting, task lighting for key transition zones (e.g., hallway to living areas); balance glare control with sufficient illumination to support edge and obstacle detection.

Mobility Strategies
- Referral to Orientation and Mobility (O&M) assessment may be appropriate to tailor scanning strategies, route planning, and safe community mobility in the context of peripheral field loss.
- O&M training may include strategies for safer, quieter navigation in busy or unfamiliar environments; avoid scripted routines and tailor to individual needs.

Task Adaptations
- Consider practical adaptations to daily tasks that rely on spatial awareness and contrast:
  - Consistent placement of frequently used items to reduce search time.
  - High-contrast labels or markers for commonly used objects and switches.
  - Tactile or auditory cues where appropriate to locate items without reliance on peripheral vision.

Assistive Technology / Non-visual Strategies
- Explore non-visual supports when appropriate:
  - Enhanced lighting, large-print or high-contrast displays for essential information.
  - Auditory alerts or talking devices for safety-critical tasks (e.g., medication reminders, door alarms) if relevant to daily routine.
  - Encourage consistent use of available magnification or lighting aids in tasks requiring near work when vision fluctuates.

Education and Support
- Provide information to patient and family about safety strategies for home navigation, recognizing that driving is restricted at present and alternative transport will be needed.
- Discuss planning for progressive changes in vision, recognizing fluctuating symptoms or progression; emphasize proactive environmental adjustments and safety cues.
- Ensure clear communication with the eye-care team to align on follow-up plans and any impending changes in treatment that may affect function.

Clinical Follow-up / Communication
- Encourage re-evaluation of functional risks as glaucoma progresses (e.g., after Esterman assessment and specialist plan).
- Coordinate with optometry for sharing functional concerns with the patient’s care team; document any agreed environmental or mobility recommendations for handover.

Driving / Transport (education and support)
- Support transition planning: discuss reasonable alternatives to driving, access to community transport or caregiver-supported trips, and safety planning during the period awaiting formal driving assessment.

Information Gaps / Clarification to seek
- Exact driving status beyond “short daytime distances” and whether driving cessation is imminent or already in effect.
- Home environment details (layout, stairs, thresholds, presence of grab bars) to tailor environmental modifications.
- Availability of caregiver or family support for mobility and transport during driving transition.
- Patient goals and priorities regarding participation in community activities or errands beyond home independence.

Falls Risk Level
- Moderate falls risk related to progressive peripheral field loss and side-collision experiences, without a reported recent fall.

5. Falls Risk Level
- Moderate

6. Summary of Key OT Considerations from Vision Findings
- Priority: enhance safe mobility in the home and immediate environment through environmental optimization (lighting, clutter reduction, and consistent path layout) and patient education about scanning and deliberate turning.
- Consider referral to Orientation & Mobility for mobility strategy assessment and individualized scanning/route planning in cluttered or unfamiliar settings.
- Implement task-adaptation ideas focused on stable, high-contrast, and consistently organized environments; use non-visual cues and high-contrast labeling where feasible.
- Develop a practical transportation plan to maintain community participation during driving cessation, including exploration of alternatives and caregiver support as needed.
- Align with ophthalmology follow-up to monitor progression and adjust safety guidance as vision changes.
- Schedule follow-up to reassess functional impact after specialist plans (e.g., surgical options or therapy) and post-environmental modifications.

- Key insight about strong connectivity:
  - Since the line provides forward edges only (i -> i+1), to travel from right side of a cut to the left side, we need an added edge that crosses that cut in the leftward direction (i.e., an edge from a white vertex on the right of the cut to a black vertex on the left of the cut).
  - For a cut placed after position t (between t and t+1), let:
    - F(t) be the number of whites with position ≤ t.
    - B(t) be the number of blacks with position ≤ t.
    - Whites on the right of the cut are indices i > F(t), blacks on the left are indices j ≤ B(t) (by black-ordering).
  - An added edge from white i to black π(i) crosses the cut iff π(i) ≤ B(t) and w_i > t.
  - Therefore, for the cut to be crossable, there must exist some i with w_i > t and π(i) ≤ B(t).

- A commonly proposed necessary condition (which is not sufficient) is to require π(i) ≤ B(w_i - 1) for all i, i.e., the i-th white is allowed to match to a black among the first B(w_i - 1) blacks. This is too strong: it ensures the cut just before each white is crossed, but strong connectivity requires crossing for every cut, and one back edge can cross many cuts. The counterexample is provided by the test case in the prompt where the naive product approach yields 0 while the correct answer is nonzero.

- The exact counting problem reduces to ensuring that for every cut t, there exists at least one back-edge crossing that cut. This is equivalent to requiring that for every t, there exists i with w_i > t and π(i) ≤ B(t). If we denote the N white indices by i = 1..N in increasing order of their positions w_i, and the N blacks by their positions b_j in increasing order, the problem becomes: count the number of permutations π of {1..N} such that for every t, the set {i : w_i > t and π(i) ≤ B(t)} is nonempty.

- A reformulation that is often useful for such problems:
  - For t with the property F(t) = B(t) (these are the times the path of (F, B) touches the diagonal), the cut is balanced: there are i = F(t) whites on the left and i blacks on the left.
  - The cut will be crossable iff among the whites on the right (indices i+1 .. N) there is at least one white that maps to a black on the left (indices 1..i).
  - If F(t) ≠ B(t), then automatically there is crossability across that cut (by a simple pigeonhole reasoning), so the only potential obstruction comes from t where F(t) = B(t).

    - For every i ∈ E, there exists at least one j > i with π(j) ≤ i.
  - In other words, for each i in E, the suffix {π(i+1), ..., π(N)} must contain an element ≤ i.

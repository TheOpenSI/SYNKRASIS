Here’s why:

• Residence Time: The residence time of carbon in the atmosphere is only about 4–5 years. This means that, on average, all of the atmospheric CO₂ is replaced roughly every five years. In contrast, other pools—such as deep ocean water (which can take thousands to tens of thousands of years) or fossil fuels/sediments (with residence times spanning millions of years)—exchange carbon much more slowly.

• Fluxes: Although the atmosphere may contain less total carbon than, say, terrestrial biomass or deep oceans, the rate at which carbon enters and leaves the atmospheric pool is very high. For example, during photosynthesis, plants take up CO₂ from the atmosphere rapidly (especially on seasonal time scales), and respiration, decomposition, and combustion return it just as quickly.

• Comparison with Other Pools: Even though soils contain large amounts of carbon, much of that carbon is in forms that change very slowly over decades to centuries. The deep ocean exchanges carbon only very gradually compared to the rapid cycling seen in the atmosphere.
